## 1. EXECUTIVE DIAGNOSIS

The prose no longer fails at the **sentence** level (the naturalness repair fixed grammar, MTL residue, and forced fragments). What remains is a **description-system** issue one layer up.

Three concrete problems recur:

1. **Comparison is used as a shortcut for characterization.** When a person, room, or machine must be conveyed, the narration tends to reach for a compact comparative clause — "like someone who…", "as if…", "the way…". These are not wrong individually, but they cluster at the exact moments the reader needs *physical* cues, so the reader is given an **evaluation instead of an image**.

2. **Scenes are closed with a thematic summary sentence.** A number of paragraphs end on an interpretive/thesis beat ("he was looking at the thing the Academy did…"; "That, he understood, was the point."). This is TNE/economy *structure* surfacing as an authorial voice: it tells the reader what to conclude instead of letting the concrete details carry it. Two of these (CH002) are stacked with anaphora ("None of them… None of them…"; "It did not… It did not…") and read as deliberately "literary."

3. **Atmosphere is sometimes asserted rather than shown.** Where a place needs to be felt, the text occasionally uses an abstract bucket ("full of ordinary August noise"; "The room was not frightening… not even cold"; "the basement was his") rather than one or two concrete anchors the reader can picture.

**Spatial and temporal clarity are generally good.** With one or two minor exceptions, the reader always knows where Arthur is, where objects sit, and when the scene occurs. **No passage is a canon error, a continuity error, or an English error** — hence zero MUST FIX.

**Diagnosis in one line:** the remaining issue is *descriptive framing* — interpretation and thesis arriving **before** or **instead of** a concrete image — not grammar, not clarity of fact, and not scene logic.

## 2. DESCRIPTION SYSTEM DIAGNOSIS

Recurring generation patterns observable across the four chapters:

- **Pattern 1 — Comparison-as-characterization.** Similes/“as if” clauses land on people and institutions at high frequency (Margaret, Hayes, the compliance clerk, the examiner). Function is usually characterization or mood; the cost is that physical specificity is skipped.
- **Pattern 2 — Thesis closure.** Paragraphs frequently end on an abstract/thematic sentence. This is the most visible TNE-structure residue: a "smart" closing line that summarizes meaning.
- **Pattern 3 — Abstract bucket nouns.** "noise," "quiet," "order," "routine," "weight," "presence," "atmosphere" appear as description where a concrete noun would visualize better.
- **Pattern 4 — Object/institution personification.** Objects, files, rooms, and stones are given intent ("the walls played back conversations"; "a wrong file had a way of becoming invisible"; "the direction it wanted him to look"; "the paperwork lived"). Mild, but systemic.
- **Pattern 5 — Stacked beats.** Some single beats compress object + metaphor + interpretation + conclusion (worst: CH002 L58 and L66). The reader gets the *meaning* of a scene before the *picture* of it.
- **Pattern 6 — Declarative sparse place intros.** Some locations get one declarative line ("The mess was a long room with a low ceiling") and are otherwise unvisualized — under-described relative to their narrative weight.

Net: description is **usable but leans interpretive**. It rarely fails outright; it repeatedly chooses the shortcut.

## 7. RECURRING FAILURE PATTERNS

1. **Comparison-as-characterization** (F2-02, F2-07, F4-03, plus "the way" instances noted in the prior audit). Simile clauses substitute for a behavioral cue.
2. **Thesis/anaphora closure** (F2-04, F2-05, F3-04, F3-03). Scene meaning is summarized; anaphora marks it as engineered.
3. **Abstract/negative atmosphere** (F1-01, F1-03, F2-01, F2-03). Mood asserted via absence or a bucket noun instead of one concrete anchor.
4. **Object/institution personification** (F1-06, F4-01, F4-02, F4-04). Files, walls, paperwork, and the stone are given intent.
5. **Stacked description beats** (F2-04, F2-05; milder F1-04, F3-01). Physical + metaphor + interpretation + conclusion compressed into one paragraph.

## 8. CONCRETE REPLACEMENT RULES

Practical generation rules that would address every pattern above:

1. **Describe the object before interpreting it.** Give one physical anchor (shape, material, position, sound, temperature) before any meaning.
2. **One physical anchor per descriptive paragraph.** At least one concrete noun the reader can picture.
3. **Do not use metaphor when direct physical description is clearer.** Reserve comparison for what cannot be shown (mood, abstraction, scale).
4. **No thesis closure.** Do not end a paragraph on what the scene "means" or "was the point" — let concrete details carry it.
5. **Ban the anaphora summary** ("It did not… It did not…"; "None of them… None of them…") as a scene-ending device.
6. **Prefer behavior to simile for people.** Show a physical action/tell instead of "like someone who…".
7. **Keep personification literal-safe.** Objects may be *felt* (warm, leaning, humming); they may not *know*, *want*, *play back*, or *become invisible*.
8. **Replace atmosphere buckets** ("noise", "quiet", "presence", "atmosphere") with one concrete source (a sound, an object, a movement).
9. **State location relations when a character moves.** If an object was placed earlier, restate its proximity on re-use.
10. **Interpretation after observation, never instead of it** — observation → inference, in that order, per paragraph.

These are already consistent with the proposed **Description Repair Principles** from the authority audit; no new canon or engine concept is required.
