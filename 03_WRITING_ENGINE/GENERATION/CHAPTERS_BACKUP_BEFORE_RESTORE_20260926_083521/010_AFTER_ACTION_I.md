# AFTER-ACTION I

The review met two days later, after the morning Ravenscroft Academy block, in a room on the second floor with a long table and a screen that did not turn on.

Arthur was there because the file was there. The file was the corrected one, and the corrected one had the occurrence log folded inside it and his own method log behind that, and the method log was why a Support cadet had been asked to sit at the end of the table at all. He was not asked anything for the first hour. That was correct. The review was not of him.

The panel was three officers and a clerk. The senior presented the operation, which took nineteen minutes and did not use the word *lucky* once, and the panel asked him the questions a panel asks: why the site was held silent for two hours, whether the hold had been authorized, what the cost of the hold had been to the work order, and whether the same result could have been reached faster. The senior answered the last one by putting the method log on the table and opening it to the fifth of August, and the panel read a page of Arthur's handwriting, and the room went quiet in the particular way a room goes quiet when a dull document turns out to be right.

That was the whole of it. Dates, times, and one column of what had happened, in the plainest words he had. It was accurate. It was also, Arthur knew, the dullest document in the folder, and that was not an accident he had planned, only a thing he had not fixed, and he had watched enough rooms to know that a page like that one did not get read so much as survived.

The panel read it. The panel asked the senior two more questions about the hold. Then the clerk wrote a word in a box, and the review was graded, and the grade was for the operation, which was the team's, and the team's conduct was marked *sound*, and the room moved on.

---

The review was filed that afternoon.

Arthur filed it, because filing the after-action was the Support cadet's work, and he did it the way he did all of it, by the numbers on the spine. The review ran to five sections, which was the number reviews ran to. The team's section credited the team. The section on the operation credited the hold, and the senior, and the two hours of a road made quiet by four people standing still on it. The cohort's name went into the reputation ledger the same day, and the ledger said what ledgers say, which is numbers, and the numbers were up.

His own name was not in any of the five sections.

His log was.

It went in behind the review, as an attachment, because the senior had put it on the table and the panel had read it, and a thing read by a panel becomes part of what the panel read. That was all. There was no commendation and no consequence. A sheet that said a log was complete had noticed the log and nothing else. It had been put in front of the review and not acted on. It would sit in the folder with the rest of the read-and-unread and wait, as paper waits, for someone who wanted to read it.

---

The cohort's credit went up on the board in the east block the next morning, which was how the Academy did praise: a sheet on a board, a name and a number, and everyone who passed it deciding what the number meant.

The same morning, the training hall was already using EVENT-100 as an example. An instructor had drawn the site on the board and asked the students what they would do if a phenomenon repeated the last sound it heard. Three answers involved force. One involved evacuation. Nobody mentioned silence until the instructor pointed at the operation log and asked why the team had stopped working.

Arthur was halfway through the doorway when the instructor's eye found him.

"Support cadet."

He stopped.

"What did the team know before it acted?"

Arthur answered with the log's simplest fact. "What the phenomenon repeated."

The instructor nodded and moved on. No praise. No special treatment. Just another answer in a class that would use the incident again tomorrow.

The team's names were on the sheet. The senior's was first, as it always was, and the two students under him ÃƒÂ¢Ã¢â€šÂ¬Ã¢â‚¬Â the one from Combat and Pram ÃƒÂ¢Ã¢â€šÂ¬Ã¢â‚¬Â and the Recon, Medical, and Tactical students were under that, and the sheet said *sound* beside the operation and *plus* beside the cohort, and for one morning the cohort was a cohort that had done something. The dining hall was loud that night in a good way. Arthur ate at his table near the bins and listened to the room be pleased, which was not the same as being pleased, and was not the same as being left out, because he had a table and the table was his and the room's good night could not be taken from him.

His name was not on the sheet. A Support cadet's name did not go onto an operation's sheet; the sheet was for the team that had held the road, and he had not held the road, he had read a file, and the file had gone into the folder with the review, which was a different sheet in a different room.

He did not mind it, or did not mind it much. What he minded, a little, was that the number beside the cohort was a number, and numbers went into ledgers, and no ledger had a line for a log.

That changed two days later, in a way no ledger would have shown.

---

Two days after the review, the archive gave him a key he had not asked for.

It was not a promotion and it did not look like one. It was a change to his access line, typed by the same clerk who typed everything, and the change said that Student Records Grade 3 was now Student Records Grade 3, stacks two and below, and that the request was to see the clerk at the desk for the stairs. Stacks two and below were the deeper stacks ÃƒÂ¢Ã¢â€šÂ¬Ã¢â‚¬Â the ones off the public floor, where the archive kept what it kept; a Support cadet with a Grade 3 line did not go there. A Support cadet with a line that said *stacks two and below* did.

He went to the desk and the clerk gave him the stairs and said the thing the clerk said to everyone who got the line, which was nothing. He did not go down. There was nothing he needed to fetch that afternoon and no reason to walk a new corridor to prove a corridor existed. He put the access line in his log with the rest of the numbers, because it was a number, and went back to the low pile, which was still his, and still waiting, and still had four shelves of it.

He did not tell anyone. There was no one to tell who would have understood what had changed. The review had said *sound* and put a number in a ledger, and the ledger had gone up for the cohort, and none of that had anything to do with a clerk's access line, except that it did, and the connection was exactly the kind of connection the institution made without meaning to: a log had been noticed, and a notice had become a line, and a line had become a door.

The door was not a rank. He knew it was not a rank because his rank had not changed and would not. He was still Rank F, still Irregular, still filing the Support equipment room. The new access was still useful. But it was not a promotion. The archive had written it down, and on the record it would stay written.

That evening, before he left, he stood a moment at the head of the stairs to the deeper stacks and looked down them, and then went up to the low pile instead, because the low pile was where his work was, and the deeper stacks would still be there in the morning, and a door did not have to be walked through the day it opened.

He had not been promoted. He had been believed.

That night, alone in the archive, Arthur opened the interface.

For the first time, another line was present.

He read it twice.

The words were different from **NO CONTRACT**.

Not an offer.
Not yet.

Something had changed because of what he had done, but the interface did not tell him what.

Arthur closed it.

If it wanted to become clearer, it could wait.

So could he.

The next morning, the Academy removed the case from routine training.

Someone had requested the original recording.

Arthur had not.
