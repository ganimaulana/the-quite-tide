# ERROR / UNDEFINED

One week after the assessment, Arthur Reed stood outside Ravenscroft Academy and watched several hundred new students pass through the gates.

The Academy stood in Ravenhurst City's Academy Quarter, a long belt of schools, laboratories, hospitals, and training facilities on the northern side of the coastal city. From the hill, Arthur could see the city falling toward Raven Bay: apartment blocks, office towers, older streets packed between newer roads, and the pale line of the sea beyond them.

Ravenhurst was not unusual because it had an Academy for people with unusual abilities. In the Kingdom of Albion, Esper education had become part of ordinary public life. The word appeared on school forms, medical records, university applications, and the signs outside specialist clinics.

Some people could channel forces that ordinary physics did not explain. They were called Espers in everyday speech, Attuned in official terminology. Other things were harder to place. A room that changed its exits. An object that followed a rule no engineer had designed. A phenomenon that appeared, repeated itself, and refused to behave like an accident.

Those were Anomalies.

Some were harmless. Some were dangerous enough that people called them monsters, even when the official file used the colder word.

People had learned to live with both.

Arthur had learned to keep his expectations modest.

His admission sheet was folded inside his jacket. Rank F. Irregular. Operations Support. Records and Archives.

None of those words bothered him as much as the one question he had not been able to answer since the assessment.

Why had the machine stopped?

Seven days earlier, he had been sitting inside a district assessment room when the machine had hesitated for three beats before giving him a rank.

He had noticed because nothing else in the room had hesitated for even one.

By the time they called his name, he had already counted forty-one students through the assessment room.

He had not started counting on purpose. The line moved at a steady rate, one student every few minutes. After the first ten, he began checking the rate against the clock to see whether it held. It did. The intake was efficient, and that told him more than the district-office pamphlet, which had been mostly photographs.

The room was long and plain. Pale walls, no windows on the far side, a strip of light along the ceiling. At the near end, three chairs faced a recessed bay where the wall had been cut back and fitted with equipment he could not see from the line. At the far end, an examiner sat behind a console, and the console was angled so that only the examiner could read it.

Students went up one at a time. They sat. The bay hummed, low, more felt than heard. The examiner watched the console and not the student. A printer clicked, a slip came out, and the student was sent down the side corridor with it.

No one looked pleased or afraid. That was the part Arthur kept watching. The room was not frightening. It was not even cold. Nothing about it suggested that anything could go wrong.

A boy came back past him holding his slip, and Arthur did not turn to read it. He noted the grip, though. People held paper differently depending on what it said.

Since waking after the incident, he had been noticing movement more clearly than other people seemed to.

During the weeks before the Academy, he had finally given the ability a name of his own: **Motion Sense**.

It was not impressive. He could tell when something moved without looking directly at it. He could follow a thrown object more easily than other people. A person crossing his peripheral vision seemed to leave a cleaner trail in his awareness than a stationary person did.

That was all.

It did not tell him why something moved. It did not tell him what someone intended to do. A person standing still was no easier to read than anyone else's.

The assessment had also separated three things the paperwork treated as different: Esper Rank, the Academy Performance Record, and what a person could actually do with a body. Rank F described his measurable conventional Esper output. It did not describe his physical condition, and it did not predict his future performance.

He had spent an evening testing the limits with ordinary things: a coin dropped from different heights, a curtain moved by the window, a chair moved across the floor while he looked at the wall. He could detect the movement. He could not make the movement mean anything.

He had written the results down anyway.

**Movement: clear. Meaning: none.**

That was why he had never considered Motion Sense a power worth talking about.

"Next."

He crossed the room and sat.

The chair was plain metal and did not adjust. He put his hands on his knees and waited.

The procedure was quieter than he had expected. He had braced for something like a medical scan, a cold contact, a spike of noise. Instead there was only the hum and a faint pressure behind his eyes that came and went.

He watched the examiner's face, because the examiner was the only part of the procedure that could tell him anything. He did not look at the console. He could not have read it from where he sat even if he had tried, and trying would only have drawn attention to him.

For a while nothing happened. Then the examiner stopped typing.

He frowned. Not at Arthur. At the console.

He typed again, slower. The frown stayed. He pressed a button on a small panel and said, to no one Arthur could see, "Seven point three."

A second person came in through a door Arthur had not noticed, a woman in the same grey, carrying a folder. She stood at the console and read it over the examiner's shoulder. Neither of them spoke to Arthur or looked at him. Whatever was happening was happening inside the machine, and neither of them saw any reason to involve him in it.

She signed something. The examiner signed something. A printer clicked.

The slip came out, and the examiner read it before Arthur did. Something crossed his face and was gone before Arthur could read it. He folded the slip in half, set it on the counter, and said, "Take this to the next desk."

Arthur took it.

He read it in the corridor, where the light was better.

RANK: F

NATURAL RESPONSE: MOTION SENSE

CLASSIFICATION: IRREGULAR (PROVISIONAL)

Below that was a blank box for a reviewer's stamp.

He stood there for a moment, holding the slip. The F did not surprise him. Motion Sense had never moved anything, and the machine was not wrong about that. It was a perception ability with almost no direct output. Useful, perhaps, if a person knew what to do with movement. Not useful in the way the Academy usually meant useful.

It was not the slip that stayed with him. It was the pause before it.

The reading had not come out all at once. It had stalled. For three slow beats, longer than any student before him, the console had done nothing. Then it had returned a different answer.

Three beats was not much. Nobody in the room had seemed to notice, but he had. Motion Sense did not explain the pause; it only made him notice the small movements around it more clearly: the examiner's fingers stopping, the second clerk shifting her weight, the printer waiting before it clicked.

He folded the slip as the examiner had and put it in his pocket.

---

The district processing office was three days later, small and bright, with the smell of paper and floor wax.

He waited on a bench until a clerk called a number, then sat at the counter while the clerk opened his file and read the top of it out loud, in the flat voice people use for forms.

"Subject, age seventeen. Classification, Irregular."

Arthur did not react. He had learned the word the week before and had read what he could find about it, which was not much and most of it old.

"Division, Operations Support." The clerk turned a page. "Assignment, Records and Archives."

"Records and Archives," Arthur said.

"That's what it says."

He let that sit. He could see a practical logic in it. Operations Support was the division; Records and Archives was the assignment. The Academy had not put him outside operations. It had put him at the edge of them, where paper had to match the work it described.

For now, that meant a shelf.

The clerk in front of him had no opinion about any of this. He was simply good at his job, and his job was to move Arthur through the counter. Arthur respected that more than he had expected to.

He had one question, and he spent it carefully. A question asked out of turn became a note in a file.

"Is the ERROR re-measured?"

The clerk checked. "Annual re-examination. It's on the sheet. System-generated. No manual rank changes."

It was. He read the whole sheet twice. The wording was careful. Whoever had written it had taken care not to promise him anything, and not to lie.

Some students would have argued. Arthur had a rule about arguing, one he had never written down and did not intend to. An argument made a record. He would rather keep his own.

He signed where the clerk pointed and took his copy.

On the way out he stood in the doorway for a moment with the sheet in his hand. He remembered the machine's hesitation more clearly than the result. He meant to remember the reading.

He folded the sheet and went to find a train timetable.

Seven days later, he stood at the gates of Ravenscroft Academy again.

The gate was crowded, and the crowd was divided into lines before anyone entered. A line for the standard intake, a shorter line for late registrations, and a desk off to the side for anything the two lines could not hold. Arthur read the signs once and joined the standard line, because the sheet said he was standard intake, whatever else it said about him.

The students around him were loud, most of them talking to cover their nerves. Two of them argued about the ladders, not the Esper ranks but the other ones, the operational ones, and got half of it wrong. A girl read a manual with her lips moving.

The line moved. At the desk, an officer took his sheet, glanced at it, and glanced again.

"Records and Archives," she said, to herself.

"Yes."

She typed. Nothing came up, or nothing she expected. She typed again, then said, "One moment," and went through a door behind the desk.

The boy behind Arthur leaned around him. "What'd you do?"

"Nothing," Arthur said.

"You're Irregular." He had read it off the sheet over Arthur's shoulder. His voice was not cruel. He sounded tired; he had heard the word before and had already decided what it meant. "Filing cabinets."

"Something like that."

The officer came back with a supervisor, who read the sheet twice and stamped it without any expression at all. That was Ravenscroft Academy's whole opinion of him so far: two readings and a stamp. Arthur supposed that was fair enough. The system had measured what it could measure and had not pretended to measure more.

"Annex four," the supervisor said. "Records and Archives. You report there, not to the residential block. Quarters are in the east block."

East block. Not a residential block for Irregulars. There was no such thing, he had learned. Irregular was a classification, a word on a page, not a building. The building was simply where the paperwork lived. He was to live near it.

He took his stamped sheet and walked up the hill.

East block was long and low and set back from the main hall. By the time he reached it, the crowd had thinned to almost nothing. The annex sat at the end of a covered walk, a plain door, with a plate beside it whose letters, RECORDS AND ARCHIVES, had dulled with weather.

He stopped at the door.

On the walk up, he had been thinking about the students he had counted through the assessment room before him, who had come out with a rank and an ability and a classification and no pause, no second person, no countersign. He still did not know what separated him from them, only that it was not a gift.

He came back the next morning and opened the door, because standing in front of it was not going to answer anything.

Inside, the archive smelled like paper and dust and cold air. It was enormous, and it was quiet, and every shelf was full.

He reported in. He was given a locker, a badge with a number, and a slip of his own to file.

He put the slip where the system said it went. Then he stood in the aisle for a moment, listening to the quiet. Somewhere down the corridor, a cart rolled past. Motion Sense caught it before he turned his head.

The instrument had hesitated, and he meant to find out why.

For a few seconds, the corridor remained exactly as it had been.

Then the words appeared.

________________________________________
CONTRACT INTERFACE INITIALIZING

USER
ARTHUR REED

NATURAL ABILITY
Motion Sense
Status: ACTIVE

CONTRACT SYSTEM
Status: NO CONTRACT

WARNING
System information may be incomplete.
________________________________________

Arthur stopped.

The words were not on the wall. They were not on his badge, the archive terminal, or the glass of the office door. He looked over his shoulder.

Two clerks passed the end of the aisle without slowing.

Only he could see it.

The memory of the assessment room came back in pieces: the machine stopping, the examiner's fingers freezing over the console, the printer waiting before it finally produced his result.

The interface did not explain the pause.

It did not explain ERROR / UNDEFINED either.

It simply existed.

Arthur read the three words again.

**NO CONTRACT.**

So whatever this was, it had not given him anything.

That made the problem smaller.

He closed his eyes.

When he opened them, the display was gone.

He did not tell Margaret.
He did not tell the Academy.

He went back to the shelf.
