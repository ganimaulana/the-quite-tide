# MIDPOINT HOOK

**Scene one â€” the appraisal request.**

The Institute's offer arrived in a different form. Not a letter. A meeting.

Arthur was brought to a room with a long table and four officials. Dr. Kajiwara was there, but she did not speak. The official at the head of the table did.

"We would like to appraise the artifact," he said.

"It has been appraised before," Arthur said. "In 1987. The appraisal said it was useless."

"That appraisal was wrong. We know it was wrong. We would like to do better."

"How?"

"By separating it from you for a short time. A controlled study. You remain the owner. We remain the observers."

Arthur looked at Dr. Kajiwara. She looked at the table.

"What if it goes dormant in other hands?" Arthur asked.

The official smiled. "Then we will know it chooses you."

"And if it does not go dormant?"

"Then we will know it is a tool, and you are holding it." The official folded his hands. "Either result tells us something. That is the purpose of an appraisal."

Arthur read the terms on the table in front of him. There were eleven pages. The pages covered liability, custody, duration, and compensation. None of them covered what happened to the artifact if the appraisal damaged it, because the Institute did not believe it could be damaged.

He signed nothing. He asked for a copy of the terms, which he was given, and which he read that night in the records block, three times.

On the third reading, he found the clause. It was on page nine, in the paragraph about duration: *The subject may withdraw from the study at any time. Withdrawal does not affect custody of the object.* That sounded generous. It was not. It meant that if he withdrew, the Institute would still hold the object for the duration of the withdrawal review, which the terms did not define.

He underlined the clause. Then he wrote, in the margin, the question he would ask at the next meeting: *How long is a withdrawal review?*

It was the first time he had found a gap in an Institute document. It told him something useful: the Institute wrote its terms the way it wrote its files. The important thing was never missing. It was simply filed under a heading that looked ordinary.

**Scene two â€” the separation.**

They put the artifact in a warded case. The case was held in a room with glass walls. Arthur stood on one side. The officials stood on the other.

For the first minute, nothing happened.

For the second minute, the case grew warm.

For the third minute, the Seep they had brought â€” the one from the warehouse â€” stopped returning objects. It went quiet.

"It is dormant," the official said.

"No," Arthur said. "It is refusing."

The official looked at him. "Refusing what?"

"To show you anything without me."

Arthur put his hand on the glass. The Seep moved again. The artifact warmed. The case stayed closed.

"It does not separate," the official said. "Not while anyone is watching."

"It does not separate while I am not there," Arthur said. "That is different."

They tried again. This time they cleared the observation room and left only instruments. The artifact sat in the case. The Seep sat in its warded box. For six hours, the instruments recorded nothing: no warmth, no movement, no flicker.

When Arthur returned, the artifact was warm again, and the Seep had moved.

The official wrote for a long time. Arthur watched the pen and understood that the experiment had produced the one result the Institute could not use: the artifact did not do anything on its own. It did things through the keeper. Which meant the keeper could not be replaced by a case, a room, or an instrument.

"Again," the official said. "With a second observer present."

They ran it a third time, with the junior official alone in the room and Arthur behind the glass. The artifact stayed cold. The Seep stayed still. After two hours, the junior official reported a headache, and the run was stopped.

"There is a variable we have not controlled," the official said.

"There is a variable you cannot control," Arthur said. "The keeper."

"Then we will control the keeper."

The sentence sat in the room for a moment. It was the first time anyone had said it out loud, in a formal setting, with witnesses and instruments running. Arthur wrote it down verbatim, because a sentence like that was a fact, and a fact spoken in front of four officials and a recording instrument was a fact that could not be unsaid.

**Scene three â€” the new posture.**

After the separation failed, the Institute's attitude changed. The offer became less polite. The language shifted from "research attachment" to "cooperative study" to "arrangement in the public interest."

Dr. Kajiwara came to the Academy the next morning with the rewritten terms.

"They are going to keep the original offer open," she said, "and add a second one beside it. The second one does not require you to leave the Academy. It requires you to accept an Institute liaison in your working life, full access to your files, and the right of first refusal on the artifact."

"That is not an offer. That is a takeover."

"It is procedural." She set the papers down. "They have decided they cannot separate you from the artifact. So they will separate the artifact from its privacy."

Arthur read the second offer. It was well written. It did not ask for the parallel file by name. It asked for "all working notes related to the appraisal subject," which was the same thing in a wider net.

"Has the board read the appendix?" Arthur asked.

"They have. They found it useful."

"Then they already have the method."

"They have the method as it was written in November." Dr. Kajiwara looked at him steadily. "They want the method as it is written now. The November version describes the thimble. The current version describes the thimble, the warehouse Seep, the ferry district, and the artifact's role in all three. That is four times the value."

"So they are not buying the artifact. They are buying the time between November and now."

"Yes. That is the whole offer. They want the two months you have not published."

Arthur looked at the papers. Two months. In two months he had found the Reed line, the two hands, the 20 September date, the missing bearer, and the possibility that he was in the line by assignment rather than birth. The Institute wanted all of it, in a folder, on their terms.

He did not sign.

He filed every letter in the same folder. He labeled the folder **REED ARTIFACT â€” INSTITUTE CORRESPONDENCE**. Then he put it in the bottom drawer, with the other things he was not yet ready to look at.

**Scene four â€” the midpoint.**

That night, he sat on the roof of the east wing with Clarke. It had begun to snow, lightly, the kind of snow that did not stick.

"They are not going to stop," Clarke said.

"No."

"They will keep escalating until you give them something."

"Then I will give them something." Arthur opened his notebook. "I will give them the method. All of it. Published, attributed, and complete."

Clarke looked at him. "That destroys the value of taking it from you."

"Yes."

"But it also destroys your advantage."

"I do not have an advantage. I have a record." Arthur closed the notebook. "The method is already in the appendix. The Institute wants the cross-index because the cross-index is the only part that is mine. If I publish the cross-index too, then the only thing that is mine is the artifact, and the artifact cannot be taken by reading."

Clarke was quiet for a while. The snow went past them.

"You are going to give away the thing that makes you valuable," he said, "so that the only thing left is the thing they cannot buy."

"Yes."

"That is either the smartest move or the stupidest one I have ever watched."

"It is both. That is what a midpoint is."

Arthur wrote the line he had been circling all evening: **Midpoint is not the appraisal. Midpoint is what the appraisal refuses to see.**

Then he closed the notebook and waited for the next chapter.

Before he went in, Clarke asked him one more thing.

"Are you going to publish it?"

"Not yet."

"Why not?"

"Because the Institute is not the only reader." Arthur looked at the snow. "If I publish the cross-index, I publish the 20 September date, and the two hands, and the missing bearer. Those are not just facts about the artifact. They are facts about me. And there is someone whose handwriting appears in all of them, and I do not yet know whether that person is waiting for me to publish or waiting for me to stop."

Clarke thought about it. "So you are waiting for them to move first."

"I am waiting for them to file something I can read."

The snow stopped. They went inside. In the records block, the drawer held nine documents and one date, and the person who had written the date had not yet made a mistake.
