The old worker came back the next morning with a tin box under his arm. He set it on the chair in the middle of the warehouse and opened it. Inside were three things: a ferry token, a photograph of a young man in a warehouse coat, and a folded paper with a name on it.

"My brother's things," the old man said. "He left them with me when he went north. I never opened the paper."

"Why not?"

"Because he said not to."

He handed the paper to Arthur. It was the 1975 transfer slip. The name on it was Reed T., the same clerk from the employment log, the same name from the 1934 margin, the same name in the 1927 record.

Arthur laid the transfer slip beside the photograph of the ticket window. Two documents, forty-eight years apart, same family name, same two hands Ã¢â‚¬â€ the 1927 rightward hook and the 1934 leftward hook, both present again on the 1975 slip.

"He was a clerk in 1927," Arthur said. "He was a clerk in 1978. He cannot have been both."

The old man shook his head. "The clerk in 1978 was young. Thirty at most."

"Then he was born around 1948. In 1927, he would not have been born."

"Then the 1927 clerk was someone else with the same name." The old man shrugged. "Families reuse names."

Arthur had considered that. It was the ordinary explanation, and he had filed it as preferred. But the hands were not ordinary. In 1927 the clerk wrote with a rightward hook. In 1934, leftward. In 1975, rightward again. Two hands, alternating across fifty years, sharing one name.

He did not say what he suspected. He only wrote it down.

What he suspected was this: the name was not inherited. It was assigned. Two clerks fifty years apart had signed the same name with two different hands because the name was not theirs. It was the position's. The position came with a signature, and the signature moved from hand to hand the way the artifact moved from keeper to keeper.

It was a guess. He wrote it under **UNVERIFIED** and did not show it to anyone. A guess about a family name was a guess about himself, and he was not ready to file that under anything but the bottom drawer.

They ran Pram's experiment first. Arthur placed the 1927 photograph in the center of the floor and asked the team to stand around it. The Seep moved the dust, but the tools stayed at the door.

"Paper is not a witness," Clarke said.

"Or the photograph is too small a crowd," Owen said.

"Or the Seep reads people, not images," Arthur said. "Pram's experiment failed. That is a result."

Pram did not look pleased, but he did not look surprised either. "Then we use the old man."

They tried the old man's memory again, this time with the tin box open beside him. The old man stood at the door and remembered his brother leaving. For twenty minutes nothing happened. Then the Seep moved, but not toward the door and not toward the tin. It moved toward the north wall of the warehouse, and it piled every tool in the room against the wall, in a line, pointing north.

"That is not the door," Owen said.

"That is the direction his brother went," Arthur said.

The Seep was not following the old man's loss. It was following the old man's last memory of his brother, which was not the door at all. It was the north.

"Then the fingers are not here," the old man said. "They went north with him."

"I do not think so," Arthur said. "I think the Seep is not answering the question you asked. It is answering the question you are holding."

"What question?"

"Where did he go?"

The old man was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "I have asked myself that for fifty years."

Arthur looked at the tools against the north wall. A Seep was supposed to be a simple thing: a residue of place, a repetition of loss. This one was holding a man's question, which meant the question had been strong enough to leave a mark on the building. Fifty years of asking, and the building had learned to ask it too.

"Can we close it?" Pram asked from the door.

"Not today."

"That is the first time you have said that."

"It is the first time it has been true." Arthur closed his notebook. "The Seep is anchored to a question, and I do not have the answer. When I do, the tools will move, and we can frame them."

"How do you know the answer will close it?"

"Because a Seep that follows a question will stop following when the question is answered. That is the whole rule." He looked at the old man. "What was your brother's full name?"

The old man told him. It was not Reed. It was not the clerk's name either. It was a third name, one that had never appeared in any of the files, and Arthur wrote it down because it was the only new fact the day had produced.

They could not contain the Seep that day. The tools stayed at the north wall, and every frame they built, the Seep ignored. The anchor was not a place. It was a question, and a question had no corners to frame.

Arthur went back to the records block and opened what he had. The 1927 record. The 1934 margin. The 1975 transfer slip. The old man's memory. The artifact's recognition. Four documents and one witness, all pointing at the same name.

He wrote a single line across the top of the page:

**Reed T. appears in 1927, 1934, 1975, and 1978. At least two people, possibly three. All associated with the artifact.**

Then he wrote the question underneath it, the one he had not let himself write before:

**If the artifact is a position, who holds it now?**

He did not know. But he knew that the position had been held before, by people who moved north and stopped writing, and that the north was where the Institute's records ended.

That night, Dr. Kajiwara sent a dining hallage to his locker. It was one line: *The Institute has a file on the north. It is sealed. I can get you the seal number, not the file.*

He wrote back: *The seal number is enough.*

He went back to the warehouse alone, after dark, because the team's presence made the Seep behave. Alone, with the artifact in his pocket, he stood in the middle of the floor and waited.

The dust came to him first. Then the tools came off the north wall, one by one, and arranged themselves in a circle around his feet. The Seep was not attacking. It was greeting something it recognized.

Arthur took the artifact out and held it in his palm.

But nothing sealed. He checked the surface the way he had checked it a hundred times since September, and the count had not changed: one physical marker altered, eleven dry. The warmth was a response, not a binding. The stone had answered the Seep the way it had answered the assessment instrument in the first week Ã¢â‚¬â€ with attention, and no terms.

He had not intended even that much. But the artifact had recognized the Seep, and the Seep had recognized the artifact, and recognition was all that had happened. He looked at the surface once more and counted, to be certain: one physical marker had changed in September, the rest remained dry. No new Contract had been offered. No terms had been offered. Whatever the stone was doing, it had not become a Contract.

The train was nearly empty. He sat by the window and turned the artifact over in his hand, once, briefly, where no one could see. The one altered marker was warm. The eleven unchanged ones were cold. The thirteenth side, as always, gave nothing.

He thought about the clerk's two hands. He thought about the old man's brother, who had gone north with a tin and stopped writing in 1975. He thought about the careful habits his mother had kept before the massacre. Somewhere in the space between those facts was a pattern, and the pattern had a shape, and the shape was the shape of a family that had carried a position for a hundred years without telling its children.

The train reached the Academy. He put the artifact away and walked up the hill in the cold. The records block was dark. The instrument room was dark. Somewhere above him, a desk he could not see had logged a fourth flicker, but he did not know that yet, and he would not know it for weeks.
