# NO RANK EFFECT

On the morning of 4 September, the requisition form came down to the basement at ten, carried by a dining hallenger who did not want to be in the basement.

He held the paper at arm's length, as if it might be contaminated. "Reed. Records and Archives." He glanced at the rest of the sheet and gave up on it, handing it to Arthur instead. "Exceptional Aptitude Review. Report to Administration block, room two this morning. It says automatic."

"Automatic for what?" Arthur asked.

"For your file." The dining hallenger looked relieved to be finished. He turned and went back up the stairs without explaining what that meant.

Arthur read the form. It was a standard requisition, except for the line at the bottom: "Triggered by protocol for unclassifiable subject." That line had a number, and the number was not one he had learned yet. He copied it onto his own sheet, because copying cost nothing.

Margaret stood at the head of the aisle and watched him read it.

"Tokurei," she said.

Arthur looked up.

"That's what the students call it. Exceptional Aptitude Review." She said the formal name flatly, like a diagnosis she did not believe. "They send anyone whose file doesn't close cleanly. You go in, they set you tasks, and if you do well enough they put the result in a sealed file that nobody reads."

"Does it change anything?"

"No." She turned back to her desk. "The pile is still here when you come back."

It was all the orientation he got.

---

The administration block was three floors above the basement, and the difference between the two might as well have been the difference between two countries. Three other students sat outside room two with the same requisition, all looking at the door as if it might answer questions it had no intention of answering.

A notice was taped to the wall. Arthur read it twice.

"Exceptional Aptitude Review (EAR). This review measures aptitude across Cognitive, Operational, and Anomaly dimensions. It does not measure, reassess, or revise Esper Rank. Results are sealed by default. Students receive band summaries only."

Below that, in smaller type, someone had added the Academy's doctrine sentence: "Rank measures what the Tide does through you. Aptitude measures what you do with what you notice."

Arthur stood in front of it until he had it by heart. Then he sat down.

A group of Combat students passed through the corridor on their way somewhere else. One of them slowed, read the requisition in Arthur's hand, and stopped. He was not the only one. They formed a small audience without quite deciding to.

"Tokurei," the Combat student said, loud enough for the corridor. "The ERROR gets its own exam."

Someone laughed. Arthur did not look up.

"What does it test?" the student went on. "Whether you can't do the same thing in nine different rooms?"

The laugh was louder this time. It was not cruel, exactly. It was the laughter of people who already knew how the world worked and had just seen it confirmed.

Arthur kept his eyes on the form. He had a rule about arguing, and the rule was that arguing made a record. But there was a difference between arguing and answering. He looked up at the student.

"It tests whether I notice things," Arthur said. "That's what the notice says."

The door to room two opened. A clerk in a white coat read a name. Not Arthur's. The Combat student drifted away with his friends, the spectacle over before it had begun.

Arthur stayed where he was and thought about what the student had actually given him: the name of the test, the number of rooms, and the fact that the Combat student believed nine was a large number.

The clerk read two more names, and then, at last, his own.

---

The review room was not what he had expected.

He had braced for another machine, another console, another examiner behind a screen. There was none. The room was large, plain, and lit by overhead lights that did not flicker. Nine tables formed a loose arc. Each held different materials ÃƒÂ¢Ã¢â€šÂ¬Ã¢â‚¬Â paper, maps, a sand-table, a cart of files, a sealed envelope, colored strips. There was no place for him to sit and be measured. There was only work to do.

Five adults stood along the far wall. The first was a woman in the grey uniform of the Senior Assessment Officer, Arthur guessed, from the way the others deferred to her. Two wore the blue of SMD examiners. A fourth man wore a Lantern pin on his collar. The fifth was Hayes.

Hayes did not look at him. He stood slightly apart, a proctor's clipboard in his hand, and watched the room with the flat attention of someone who had seen all of this before.

"You have until two o'clock to finish," the senior assessment officer said. "It is eleven now. Begin at station one. Proceed in order. You may not skip. You may ask for clarification once per station. Use it well."

Arthur walked to station one.

It was called "the box." Three reports described the same Seep event. The times contradicted. The witness statements contradicted. The physical evidence contradicted both. The task was printed on a card: "Construct the true account. Identify what cannot be resolved."

He read each report twice. Then he wrote: "Event occurred between 0310 and 0340. Witness A saw the secondary effect and mistook it for the cause. Witness B saw the cause and did not recognize it. The physical record is consistent if the Tide pulse came first and the sound followed after. What cannot be resolved: whether the pulse was deliberate or incidental, because neither report addresses intent."

He moved on. Stations three and four waited farther around the arc.

Station four was a reaction-and-movement drill: indicator lights, a marked floor, and a rule that the student had to move to the lit position without crossing the wrong line. Esper output was prohibited. The examiner was measuring the body the student had brought into the room.

Arthur settled his weight over the balls of his feet. Motion Sense made the changing lights easier to follow once they moved his attention from the display to the floor pattern itself. It did not tell him which light would change next. He still had to react.

The first light came on. He moved. Then the second. Then the third, faster and less predictable. He missed one change by half a step, corrected without stopping, and finished the sequence with his breathing still controlled.

The examiner checked the timing sheet.

"Fifth," she said.

Arthur looked at her.

"Fifth-fastest in the group tested this morning."

She looked back at the sheet. "No Esper output."

"None," Arthur said.

There was no congratulations. There did not need to be. The result was simply useful information: whatever Rank F meant on paper, his body was not slow.

At station five, the cart held cross-referenced files. The task was to locate a single broken reference. Arthur opened the index, followed the chain, and found a case file referenced to an incident that had closed a week before the file was opened. He set it aside and wrote the correction.

The examiner at the cart glanced at the clock.

Station six was "kill the favorite." The examiner presented a sealed incident summary and a hypothesis: "The phenomenon retreats from salt." Arthur read the summary. Then he wrote the cheapest test that could destroy the hypothesis: a bucket of seawater collected at the site before and after the event, compared. If the phenomenon retreated from salt, the pre-event sample would show no reaction; the post-event sample would. The test cost nothing but a walk.

The examiner read his answer and set the card down harder than necessary.

"You're supposed to design an experiment," the examiner said.

"I did," Arthur said.

"You were asked to falsify the hypothesis."

"I was asked to do it the cheapest way. That is the cheapest way."

The second examiner made a note.

Station seven was the clock: a problem under time pressure with stated error bars. Arthur calculated the ranges and wrote three possible answers instead of one. The third was right, but the test recorded the second because he had written it first. He had hedged under pressure, and the mistake was his.

Station eight asked what would fail first if the task continued for another eight hours. He wrote: "The analytic pattern holds. The social function fails first. I would route around people and write instead."

Station nine put five strangers around a map with no assigned leader. Arthur studied the route and the four faces, then wrote a plan. The others followed it. A Combat student read it aloud and gave the orders; Arthur did not contest him. They finished early. The examiner noted that the plan was sound and that the subject had not led.

He set the pen down.

The senior assessment officer looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at Hayes. Hayes did not return the look.

"You may wait outside for your summary," she said.

No rank effect.

He had known it before he read it, but seeing it on paper made the doctrine real. Rank F. Aptitude, in certain directions, exceptional. The two were not the same thing, and the paper did not pretend they were.

"Profile anomaly noted," the senior assessment officer said, writing the words across the sealed flap. "No action recommended at this time."

She closed the file and slid it into the drawer.

Arthur did not slow down.

He went back to the basement. The low pile was exactly where he had left it. Margaret glanced at him, glanced at the folded slip in his pocket, and returned to her work.

Arthur sat down at the shelf he had been rebuilding and took out the slip again.

Rank F. Exceptional. Exceptional, insufficient reference data. No rank effect.

The senior assessment officer added one final clarification before sealing the file. "Your Academy Performance Record will continue to accumulate separately. Exercises can change your cohort standing. They cannot change your Esper Rank."

Arthur understood the distinction immediately. The Academy could measure what he did. The classification system measured something else.

Nothing had changed. He was still in the basement, with the low pile. The sealed file with his name on it was somewhere above him, in a drawer he could not reach, and whatever it said would not be shown to him.

He put the slip with his own notes, in the back of the drawer where he kept the things that did not belong to the archive.

That afternoon, Operations Support sent him to Weapons Familiarization.

The notice was explicit about why.

**OPERATIONS SUPPORT ÃƒÂ¢Ã¢â€šÂ¬Ã¢â‚¬Â CROSS-FUNCTIONAL RECORD LITERACY.**

Arthur read the line twice.

The instructor explained it before anyone could ask.

"You are not being trained to become Weapons students," she said. "You are being trained to understand their records. If you cannot tell a deployment record from a maintenance record, you cannot audit a weapons file. If you cannot identify a weapon class, you cannot verify an authorization chain."

That made sense to Arthur.

He took a seat.

The Weapons students sat together near the front. Their uniforms were cleaner than his, their equipment better maintained, and they had the easy confidence of people who had spent years being told that their division mattered.

The first exercise was identification.

The instructor placed six weapon components on the table and gave the students three minutes to match each component to its weapon family and maintenance category.

Arthur did not know all six immediately.

He knew enough to read the labels correctly, then enough to compare the diagrams, then enough to eliminate the wrong categories.

He finished fourth.

One of the Weapons students looked over his sheet.

"Records really makes you memorize this?"

"The records are easier to understand if I know what they describe," Arthur said.

The student gave a short laugh.

"You're going to learn all that just to file paperwork?"

"Yes."

The student looked genuinely surprised by the answer.

The second exercise was harder. The class received a damaged-weapon report, a maintenance log, and a deployment authorization. One entry used the wrong weapon designation. The task was to identify which document was wrong and explain why.

Arthur noticed the mismatch before the Weapons student beside him did.

He checked the designation against the maintenance code.

"The deployment record is wrong," Arthur said.

The instructor looked at his sheet.

"Why?"

"The maintenance record identifies the weapon as the Mk-3 configuration. The deployment authorization uses the Mk-2 designation. The serial prefix matches Mk-3."

The instructor checked the original documents.

She looked up.

"Correct."

Arthur waited.

The instructor gave him a direct nod.

"Good work, Arthur. You caught a real records error."

He received the praise without trying to make anything of it.

The Weapons student beside him looked at the documents again.

"You actually read the maintenance code?"

"That's the job."

The instructor heard him.

"Exactly. Operations Support does not need to beat Weapons students at Weapons. It needs to understand enough Weapons to keep Weapons records accurate."

Arthur wrote that sentence down.

It was a useful rule.

The instructor was not finished.

"And understand one more distinction," she said, tapping the six components on the table. "Knowledge is not familiarization. Familiarization is not mastery."

Several Weapons students looked up.

"Knowledge means you know what you are looking at. Familiarization means you can identify it, handle it correctly, and understand what ordinary operation requires. That evening, he opened the private interface again.

NATURAL ABILITY

Motion Sense
Status: ACTIVE

Basic Function:
Perceive movement and changes in motion.

There was no percentage.

No level.

No combat rating.

No number telling him how strong he was.

Arthur almost laughed.

The Academy had spent the day measuring him with bands and classifications. The private interface did not appear interested in doing the same thing.

He closed it.

The instructor moved the lesson to a short handling exercise.

Arthur was given one standard training weapon and shown the basic safe grip. He copied the movement carefully. His first attempt was controlled but slow. His second was cleaner. A Weapons student beside him completed the sequence faster without looking at the reference card.

Arthur noticed the difference.

Knowing the correct grip was not the same as having the movement in his body.

That was the first practical distinction he had been given all morning, and he found it more useful than the terminology.

The instructor watched his second attempt.

"Knowledge is doing the right thing on paper," she said. "Mastery is still doing the right thing when the paper is gone."

Arthur looked at the training weapon in his hand.

He understood the point.

For now, he was learning enough to understand the records.

The exam had not changed his rank or assignment. It had only added another record to the pile of things the Academy knew about him. That, he understood, was the point.
