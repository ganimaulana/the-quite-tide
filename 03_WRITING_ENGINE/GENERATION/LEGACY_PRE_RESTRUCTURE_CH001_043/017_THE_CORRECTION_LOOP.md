Two days after the annex, the mark was still there, and Arthur did the thing he did with any problem he could not put down: he carried it to the one place in the building that read things for a living.

The infirmary sat at the east end of the covered way and smelled of alcohol and floor polish and, faintly, of the tea the duty staff kept going all day. He stood in the short line and said the word *burn* to the clerk at the desk, because it was the truest short word he had, and because a person who opens with a long word at a medical desk gets a long wait.

The Medical student was on the ward when he came through. The student looked up, saw him, and did not look surprised, which meant either that they had expected him or that they had been thinking about his hand since the dining hall. They finished what was in front of them, pointed him at a stool, and washed their hands.

"Give it here."

He gave the hand over. The student turned it under the lamp, the thin dark line from the base of the thumb to the wrist, and pressed beside it with a thumb, and asked the questions in the order a person asks them when the questions are a form. When did it appear. Two days ago, in the morning. Did it hurt. No. Had he burned it on anything. Possibly; he had done the floors that week, and the water in the boiler room ran hot enough to take the skin off. Was there heat, blistering, swelling. No.

The student was quiet at the last answer and turned the hand again.

"It does not look like a burn," the student said.

"No," Arthur said.

"It does not look like much of anything. No heat, no edge, no swelling. Skin's not raised." A thumb pressed the line once more. "If I saw this on a chart and nothing else, I would call it a scar you have had for years."

"It is two days old."

"I know." The student let the hand go and looked at the ledger on the trolley, and then at the line again, and Arthur watched them do the most human thing in the room, which was to turn a strange thing into a familiar one so that it would fit on a page. "Contact burn," the student said, half to him and half to the book. "No blister. Clean line. Off the boiler, you said."

"That is close enough," Arthur said, and it was, and they both knew it was not, and the student wrote it in the column where contact burns went.

The student dressed it with a strip of gauze that did nothing, told him to come back if the skin broke, and did not say whether the entry in the book was the whole of what had been written.

Arthur did not ask. He walked out past the line and down the covered way with the gauze loose on his hand, and by the time he reached the annex he understood that he had been protected by the exact thing he had spent two years of his life being: a fact that fit the form.

Hayes's office was on the second floor, had one window and no view worth the name, and the meeting had been on the notice board for a week in the flat print the faculty kept for things that were not worth a conversation.

The subject was the field-protocol module, the short course that ran before the winter exercises, and it ran for students whose division sent them into the field. Arthur's division did not. He had known that before he climbed the stairs, and had put his name on the interest list anyway, because the list existed and he could read a form, and because reading a rule is the only part of a rule anyone lets a records body have.

Hayes did not look up when he came in. He read the paper on the desk a while longer with the flat, unhurried patience of a man who had this conversation on the hour, and then he looked up.

"Your name." He turned the sheet so Arthur could see it, Arthur's own name in Arthur's own hand on the interest list, and above it, in red ink, one line. "You are aware the module is for deployables?"

"I read the standing order," Arthur said. "It says the module is open to any student whose duties may place them in contact with a live anomaly. Files reach field units. I move files."

"Files reach field units in a case," Hayes said, and set the pen down across the line. "You are not a case. You are the clerk who shelves it. Field protocols are for deployables."

"That is the same sentence twice."

"It is the same rule twice. That is what a rule is for." Hayes slid the paper back across the desk. "You may re-file the interest form under the division's field-deployment track, when your division opens one, if it opens one. Until then the answer is the standing order." He picked up the next sheet. "Records, student. It is not an insult. It is a column."

Arthur took the paper. There was no argument in the room that could beat the one on the desk; Hayes had not said a single untrue thing, or even an unkind one. He had put Arthur in the wrong column and read it aloud. It was the colder kind of contempt, because it did not have to think about him at all.

He went down to the annex and did not go home.

Then he did to the failure the only thing he had ever known how to do to a hard thing, which was to treat it like a filing.

He ruled the page into two columns, because the clerk's whole art is separating what you saw from what you decided, and under one heading he wrote what he had observed and under the other what he had assumed, and he did not let the columns touch.

**Observed.** Object is a small brass thimble. Returns to the returns tray three times: twice the morning after being removed; once after being shelved and watched for a whole shift. Returns, in each case, to the tray Arthur works, which is the tray he removes it from. No other disturbance.

He read the columns twice, and the distance between them sat on the page and did not go away. The observed column was three events and a habit of trays. The assumed column was a whole mechanism, and none of it had been in the observed column when he built it. He had gone from three meetings to a law of movement without passing through a single test, in one evening, and the reason he had gone so fast was the third thing on the bench, which was not a column.

The term. *It is bound to the hand, and not to the name.*

He looked at it a long time.

The sentence was not evidence. He understood that now with a clarity that had no comfort in it. It was the answer to a question he had asked, and it came back wearing the exact authority he had learned to feel and had never learned to weigh, and he had spent two pages treating its certainty as a result. He rebuilt the method on a fourth sheet. It took him an hour, and it was the smallest piece of work he had ever been proud of.

The rule at the top was one line: *Observation first, interpretation second, and never once as one act.* Under it he wrote the steps he would use from then on. List what was seen, in the plainest words available, and let it stand alone. List every reading the thing would support Ã¢â‚¬â€ not the first reading and not the best reading but all of them, side by side, until the plainness of the list embarrassed the part of him that wanted to be quick. Then choose, and mark the choice as a choice, in writing, in his own hand, so that a later clerk Ã¢â‚¬â€ even if the later clerk was only him Ã¢â‚¬â€ could find it and know it had been made.

It was not a clever rule, or even a new one. It was the method he already had, said out loud with the shortcuts removed; the only thing he had added was a written record of where the shortcuts went, and that was most of the distance between a method and a habit.

He tested it on the only thing he had: the thimble's three returns. The test cost him nothing; the object was in his drawer and had never let him spend anything.

He put the returns in the observed column and looked at them without deciding anything. The object went back to the tray *he* worked. He had taken it up; the clerk on the late shift had taken it up; only one of the two had ever got it back. That agreed with his first reading, and because he no longer trusted a reading that agreed with itself, he wrote the other readings out beside it.

If the term meant the hand that *accepted* Ã¢â‚¬â€ then the late clerk should have kept it, and the late clerk had not.

If the term meant the hand that *first received* Ã¢â‚¬â€ then it should never have gone anywhere but home to him, and it had gone into the clerk's tray once, in the file, before it came back.

He sat with the three of them and did not choose. That was the correction, and it was the first time in a week that being unsure had felt like work instead of failure.

Then he noticed the one thing that had been under all three readings from the start and that none of them explained, and he circled it once, because circling is what a clerk does with a thing he cannot yet file.

The sentence and the returns agreed with each other about the hand. They disagreed about *which* hand. He filed the four sheets in his own drawer, under his own name, and not in lost property, because some things are not the annex's. Then he turned down the lamp and let himself have the one clean fact the week had given him.

He had been wrong. He had found out where. He had built the thing that would keep him from being wrong that way again. He did not know what the thimble was; he knew how he would find out.

That, he decided, was a fair night's work.
