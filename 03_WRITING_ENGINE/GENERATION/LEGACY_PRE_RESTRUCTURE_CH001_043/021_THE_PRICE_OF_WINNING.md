# THE PRICE OF WINNING

**Scene one â€” the patronizing.**

The records block was quiet in the hour before dinner. Arthur sat at the low table in the corner where the Irregular cohort was allowed to do its paperwork, copying the exercise log into the ledger while the ink on the first copy dried. The low table was called the low table because it was lower than the others, and because the cohort that used it was lower than the cohort that did not. No one had decided that. It had simply happened, the way most things at the Academy happened: by gravity, and by the absence of anyone willing to move a table.

He had written the method the way he wrote everything: what happened, what was tried, what worked, and what was still unknown. The unknown section was short. He did not like short unknown sections. They made a thing look finished when it was not.

"Congratulations, Reed."

He looked up. A Combat student from another section stood over him, one of the ones who had watched the Irregulars move up the board without understanding how. The student had a friendly smile and a hand he did not offer to shake.

"The exercise," the student said.

"Thank you," Arthur said.

"Must be nice," the student said, "when the grading favors paperwork."

"The grading favored containment," Arthur said. "We contained it."

"Sure." The student's smile widened by a degree, which was worse than if it had vanished. "I'm not saying you didn't. I'm saying it's easier to contain a box when the box is already wrong. Makes the report look smart."

"The report was accurate."

"Right. Accurate." The student said the word the way a person holds a receipt they do not intend to keep. "Someone has to be accurate. Not everyone can be fast."

He walked away before Arthur could decide whether to answer. It was not the first time someone had said something like that in the two days since the exercise. It was the first time someone had said it directly to his face, which meant the sentiment had finished moving through the Academy and had arrived where he lived.

Clarke appeared at the table with two cups of tea. He set one in front of Arthur.

"Patronizing?" Clarke asked.

"Yes."

"They will get better at it. More specific. It will sting less once it becomes predictable."

Arthur took the tea. "You have experience."

"I am the boy who can read paper. I have a great deal of experience." Clarke sat across from him and wrapped both hands around his cup. "There are three kinds of compliments here. The first is the one you just got: a compliment that is an excuse for ranking you lower. The second is the one you will get tomorrow: a compliment that is an excuse for ranking you higher than they mean. The third is the one nobody gives, which is the true one."

"Which is which?"

"The first means they are afraid of the result. The second means they are afraid of you. The third means they have forgotten you exist."

"I would prefer the third."

"Everyone does. Nobody gets it." Clarke drank his tea. "You moved up the board. The third kind is no longer available to you."

They drank in silence. The records block was full of people who were not going to say what they meant. Two students at the next table were copying their own logs. Neither of them looked up.

**Scene two â€” Pram.**

Arthur found him in the courtyard outside the east training hall, standing with a group of Combat students who were laughing about something that required them to keep their voices low. Pram was not laughing. He was watching Arthur approach the way a person watches a door they expect to open the wrong way.

"Two hours of standing still," Pram said. It was not a question.

"Yes," Arthur said.

"That was the annex. This was the exercise. You keep winning things that are not fights."

"I keep solving things that are not fights."

"Same shape." Pram stepped away from the others. The Combat students stopped pretending not to listen. "You file a report, and people call it a victory. You read a box, and people call it a solve. Do you know what Combat students call that?"

"Paperwork," Arthur said.

"Paperwork." Pram said the word as if it were a rank lower than his boot. "Paperwork is not power, Reed. Power is what happens when the filing stops."

"The Seep in the east lane did not agree with you."

"The Seep was a training mistake."

"It was still there. It was still contained."

Pram leaned in. "Next time, I want to be there when it happens. I want to see what you do when the mistake is not a mistake."

"You want me to fail?"

"I want you to be in a fair fight." Pram's voice was even, not angry. "If you win a fair fight, I will call it a victory. If you win by filing, I will call it what it is."

Behind him, one of the Combat students said something too quiet to hear and the others laughed. Pram did not turn around. He kept his eyes on Arthur, and Arthur understood that the laughter was not part of the conversation. It was the audience.

"Then come to the next exercise," Arthur said.

"I have already requested it."

"Then you will see what happens when the mistake is not a mistake."

"I intend to." Pram's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "That is the only reason I requested it."

He walked away before Arthur could answer. The Combat students went back to their conversation, but the laughter was gone. One of them glanced at Arthur as they left, and the glance was not the sorting glance from the first week. It was a measuring glance. Something in the courtyard had changed shape.

Arthur stood in the courtyard and thought about the difference between a fair fight and the right question. Pram wanted a fair fight because he believed a fair fight would produce a true answer. Arthur had learned, in the annex, that the truest answers came from unfair questions. He did not say this. It would only have sounded like an excuse.

**Scene three â€” the trust beat.**

Clarke was waiting by the bins in the dining hall. Owen was with him, tapping his notebook against the table.

"Pram," Clarke said.

"Yes."

"He will not be the only one. The board is being revised. The next inter-division roster is being posted next week, and the Combat section has requested that the Irregular cluster be included."

Arthur looked at him. "All of us?"

"All of us." Clarke pushed his folder across the table. It was open to a page Arthur had not seen before: a list of exercises, dates, and a column of marks in Clarke's precise hand. "I copied the roster request. Pram signed it."

"Why?" Owen asked.

"Because he wants to beat us properly," Arthur said.

"That is the political placement," Clarke said. "If we win, the Combat section loses face for asking. If we lose, Pram can say he told us so. Either way, he controls the story."

"Unless we control it first," Owen said.

Arthur read the list. The exercises were the ones where the anomalies were least predictable, where the rules changed, where the field had the most variables. Pram had not chosen the ones where the Irregulars would fail. He had chosen the ones where they would fail publicly.

"These are the four hardest scenarios of the term," Arthur said.

"Yes."

"Did he pick them, or did the board?"

"The board. Pram only requested inclusion." Clarke turned the page. "But the board picked them because Pram asked. He wanted a fair test. The board wanted a visible one. They got both."

Arthur read the list a second time. Three of the four exercises had never been run at the Academy before. The fourth had been run once, six years ago, and the record of it was sealed.

"Which ability fits which scenario?" he said.

"That is the question," Clarke said.

"We need better records," Arthur said.

"We need better abilities," Owen said.

"We need to know which ability fits which minute," Arthur said. "That is the record."

Owen stopped tapping his notebook. He looked at the page in Clarke's folder, then at Arthur.

"You are already planning it," he said.

"I am already filing it," Arthur said. "The planning comes when they post the exercise numbers."

Clarke looked at him for a long moment. Then he closed the folder and pushed it toward Arthur.

"Keep it," he said.

"Why?"

"Because you are the only one who reads it for what it is, not for what it says about us."

Arthur took the folder. The pages were thin and the cover was worn, but the marks were clear. It was a map of all the ways a person could be useful without being loud: Clarke's document-reading, Owen's flicker-log, the girl's weather-reading hands, the boy's dry channel. He had known most of these facts before. He had not known that someone had written them down, or that the writing had been waiting for him.

He put the folder in his case and carried both back to the archive.

Pram's request would be filed by morning. The next exercise would not be an accident. And somewhere above the low table in the records block, a copy of the exercise log was already in the second folder, the one with his name on it, the one that other people had begun to read.
