He counted them again on a night when the annex was empty and the stone was on the bench under the lamp, and this time he did not stop at the counting.

He had counted them before. Anyone with eyes had. Twelve small hollows sat in the flat of the stone, arranged with no pattern he had ever been able to call a pattern, and he had put them in his notes back in his first week as he put everything in his notes: as a fact with no meaning attached, waiting for a meaning to arrive. Since then he had looked at them maybe a hundred times, and they had stayed what they were, which was twelve small hollows.

What had changed was not the stone. What had changed was that he now knew what a spent contract felt like. He had felt it once and had two weeks to remember it.

So he sat with a lens and a lamp and did the slow work, and the slow work was this: he set the stone under the glass and traced each hollow in turn, all twelve, and wrote down beside each one what he could see. Depth of it. Edge of it. Whether the bottom took the light the same as the walls. Whether its colour held to the bottom.

Eleven of them were the same. Smooth, shallow, and empty, the bottoms pale and flat and dry, like the inside of a cup that had never held anything. He had seen that before and had never made it a fact of its own. He made it a fact now: *eleven, empty, identical.* It went in the notes in his own hand, under the heading he had been carrying around for a fortnight and had only now earned the right to write.

The twelfth was not the same.

The twelfth was the one he had bound. He could not prove that with a chart Ã¢â‚¬â€ there was no chart, and the terms had given him none Ã¢â‚¬â€ but he could prove it as a clerk proves anything: by the one difference that mattered and the absence of any other. Eleven hollows were dry and one was not; he had used the contract once; the arithmetic had only one shape. The twelfth hollow held the thing he had done.

He sat back and let it land. Good facts did that quietly, with the floor going solid under them.

They were not decoration, but he could not yet call them Contract slots. They were physical markers in the stone. One had changed after the first Contract; the other eleven had not.

Twelve physical hollows, and no more. That was the observation. The stone had a fixed shape. Whatever that shape meant, he did not yet have evidence that it was the same thing as his private Contract capacity.

He did not know what had made the number twelve. He did not know whether the twelfth he had spent was spent forever or would come back. He knew the number, and he had learned in the last two weeks to be exact about the difference between what he knew and what he wanted to know.

He wrote the number down. Then he wrote, under it, the only honest question the number produced.

*If it holds twelve, who held the others?*

**Scene two Ã¢â‚¬â€ the library.**

The library was the one place in the Academy where his position bought him something real, and it bought him nothing fancy: a card, a shelf pass, and the right to request documents from the older holdings instead of only reading what was on display.

He had earned the card as he earned everything, which was by being useful and quiet about it. A records body who could find things was worth a card. Nobody had given it to him as a prize. It was a tool of the job, and he had never once mistaken it for a door marked with his name.

He went on the Thursday and he did not go looking for a mystery. He went looking for a value.

The stones connected to his family were not worth anything. That was the whole of the old story, and he had heard it at home for as long as he could remember: an appraiser had come out in 1987, had looked at grandfather's carvings, and had written one word on one page, and the word was *useless*, and his grandmother had never got over it. It had been a family joke and a family wound at the same time.

He had seen the paper. It lived in the drawer under the placemats, and it was short and old and had a line at the bottom that had been blacked out so hard the ink had gone through to the other side.

What he did on the Thursday was simple. He requested the appraisal records of the year 1987 for the district, on the library's older holdings, under his card, for the purposes of comparison. It was the sort of request a records body made a dozen times a season, and it asked nothing of anyone.

The clerk at the holdings desk brought him a box and left him alone with it, which was what the clerk was for, and Arthur put on the gloves the reading room provided and opened the box and went through the year as he went through everything.

It took him most of the afternoon, and it was ordinary work, and that was the point. He was not hunting. He was matching. A records body does not find a thing by wanting it; he finds it because he has already learned what it looks like.

The page was near the bottom of the third folder.

It was short and old, the same short and the same old, and it was an appraisal of *carved stones*, and at the foot of it, in the small cramped hand of a man who had been paid by the page, was the word **useless**.

He read it twice. There was no mistake in it, because there was nothing in it to make a mistake about: a date, a list of objects described as carved stones, a verdict, and a signature line. The objects were not described. The stones were not sketched. Whoever had written it had not thought them worth a sentence, and had said so in one word and gone home.

Then he turned the page over, because a clerk turns a page over.

The next leaf was a copy of the same appraisal, and it was not the same appraisal.

The same hand, the same word, the same line of objects Ã¢â‚¬â€ and at the bottom, where the original had the signature line, the copy had been struck through. Not crossed out carelessly. Removed. A strip of the leaf had been inked over until the paper under it was dark, and under the ink there was the faint ridge of a line that had been there once and was there no longer, as a word is still in a page after the page has been erased.

The Academy held a copy of the paper that had been recovered from the Reed family papers, and someone, at some point, for some reason, had taken the name off it.

He sat very still in the reading room with the gloves on and the lamp ticking and did the arithmetic he did not want to do. The stones in the family were not worth anything; that was the story. But an appraisal is not a story. An appraisal is a page in a file, and someone had gone to the trouble of putting this one in the Academy's holdings and then going over one line of it until the line was gone.

You do not erase a line about a thing that is useless.

You erase a line about something else.

He copied what he could see, which was almost nothing, and he did not copy what he could not, and he wrote the date at the top of his own page in his own hand, and under it the two facts he had, which were that the family's paper and the artifact's paper were the same paper, and that the part of it that mattered had been taken off the record by a person who had a reason to take it off.

He had come in looking for a value. He was walking out with a question that had been sitting in a library for longer than he had been alive.

He did not go to the annex that night. He went up to the east block and lay on his bunk with the light out and the radio low and let the day settle, and what settled out of it was one clean shape.

The thing on his shelf and the stones in the family archive were the same thread. He did not know how. He did not know whether the stones the appraiser had called useless were the same stones, or the same kind of stone, or only the same page in the same year, and any of those was a different life's work. But a stone with twelve hollows, one of them altered had been carried by someone before him, and whoever that had been had left the record clean behind them, and the cleanness was the loudest thing on the page. What he knew was that the two threads ran into one another, and that someone had noticed, and had reached into the record and taken a name out.

That was the shape. Not an answer. A shape, with a hole in it exactly the size of a name.

He thought about his mother's careful voice from before the massacre, asking whether there was anything he wanted to tell her, and about the fact that he would never hear that question again. He could not tell her that an old paper from the family archive was in a library three hundred kilometres away with a line missing. He could not tell anyone. The whole of it was one question and no paper he could show, which is the worst kind of fact for a records body to hold, because a fact you cannot file is a fact you have to carry.

He lay there a while and let it be that, and then he did the only thing he had ever known how to do with a thing he could not file, which was to give it a place to wait.

He got up and turned the desk lamp on low and took out a fresh sheet and wrote, at the top, the two threads, side by side, as a clerk sets two columns that are still going to meet. Under them he wrote the things he had, in the plainest words he had, and then, at the bottom, the question, and he was careful with it, because the question was the only part of this he would get to keep.

*Who took the line off the 1987 appraisal Ã¢â‚¬â€ and when?*

He read it back once. It was a good question. It was the kind of question a person could spend a life on and never finish, and he had the terrible feeling, looking at it in his own handwriting, that he had just written the first line of something that would not fit in the drawer with the rest.

Then he put the sheet in his own folder, under his own name, behind everything else, and turned the lamp off, and lay down again, and the stone sat on the shelf in the dark with its twelve hollows and one altered marker, holding a number it had held for a very long time, and somewhere under the number, waiting, was a name.

He did not have it. He had the shape of the place where it had been.
