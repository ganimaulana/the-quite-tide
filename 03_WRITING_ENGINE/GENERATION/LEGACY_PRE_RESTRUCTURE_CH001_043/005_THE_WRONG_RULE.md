# THE WRONG RULE

The next morning, Arthur had a practical classification lecture before his Records shift.

The instructor spent twenty minutes on response Rules and then gave the class three examples to classify. Arthur got two right and marked the third uncertain because the description did not contain enough information. The student beside him called that overthinking. The instructor called it the correct answer: a Rule was only useful when the evidence supported it.

By the time Arthur reached the annex, the lesson was still in his head. The folder that did not belong was the third one from the left on the second shelf.

Arthur had been working the low pile since his first day in the annex, and he had learned to trust the pattern. It was not intuition. The Seep was officially closed and officially misunderstood.

That was the worst part. A closed R-7 meant no follow-up. If the sound-bleed happened again, the next patrol would see a closed file and assume the matter was settled. Arthur had been in the archive long enough to know that a wrong file had a way of becoming invisible.

He copied the catalog number and the zone name onto his sheet. The file was **catalog #18**. The zone was **East Beach Annex, Zone 11**.

The lecture had taught him the classification. The file had shown him the consequence. Knowing a Rule in a classroom was not the same as seeing what happened when someone used the wrong one.

Margaret found him there.

She took the folder from the routing shelf, read the correction, and looked at the Seep report inside. Then she looked at him.

"R-4," she said.

"R-4," he agreed.

"Who filed it?"

"The initials on the original routing are smudged." He paused. "It could have been a clerk reading the wrong box."

She turned the folder over. The smudge was deliberate or old; Arthur had looked at it long enough to decide it could not be read, which was different from deciding it could not be known. Margaret seemed to reach the same conclusion. She put the folder back on the routing shelf.

"Compliance has to sign off on a Rule change," she said. "Even a clerical one."

"I know."

"They may not care."

"I know."

She left without telling him to stop correcting things. Coming from her, that was close to praise.

The compliance window was on the third floor of the annex, up a narrow stair that smelled of old varnish. Arthur had not been sent there before. The routing shelf usually handled itself. But a corrected Seep file with a changed Rule was supposed to be signed off in person, so he went.

The clerk behind the window was a man with ink on his fingers and a face practiced at receiving routine corrections. He took the folder, read the correction, and set it on the counter between them.

"This was already responded to," he said.

"Under the wrong Rule," Arthur said.

"The response was completed."

"Because the Seep is stationary, not an organism. The wrong team went out. They found nothing because there was nothing of that kind to find."

The clerk looked at him as if a machine had spoken out of turn. "The case is closed."

"The case is closed under the wrong response. If it happens again, the wrong team will go out again."

"It is not your job to decide which team goes out."

Arthur did not answer. He had learned that a piece of paper often got further than an argument. He took a blank correction form from the rack and filled it out in front of the clerk, line by line, copying the file number from the folder. Then he signed it and slid it across the counter.

The clerk looked at the form, then at Arthur. He took a stamp from the drawer and pressed it onto the form without reading it.

"Noted," he said.

"Files get corrected every day," the clerk said. "Most of them stay corrected. Some of them don't. The difference is not usually important."

"If the Seep recurs," Arthur said, "they will send the organism team again."

"Then they will send the organism team again." The clerk folded his hands. "Is there anything else?"

Arthur took the folder back. "No."

He left the window with the correction form in his pocket and the zone name still in his head. East Beach Annex, Zone 11. A sound-bleed that played back the wrong day's conversations.

He went back down the narrow stair, through the basement corridor, past the rows of shelves that held the low pile. He was supposed to return to the second shelf and keep sorting. Instead, he stopped at the end of the aisle and took out the folder.

East Beach Annex, Zone 11.

He said it under his breath. The words did not sound like anything special. It was a storage annex near the old waterfront, far enough from the main campus that most students never went there. The report said the sound-bleed had been noticed by a janitor in late August, logged by a patrol, and then filed under R-7 because the clerk who had logged it had checked the wrong box on the form. The box was one column over from the right one. That was all it took for a file to go the wrong way. He said the name a second time, softly, and the warmth against his side held steady.

He put the folder back on the routing shelf.

Then something moved behind the shelving.

It did not move in the way a person moves. It was a flat, heavy thing, warm as it had been since March, and now the warmth was not uniform. One side of it was warmer than the other. He watched the movement until the cart disappeared.

The movement had nothing to do with the file. He wrote that distinction down before his mind could turn it into a conclusion.

He stood very still.

A Records body was expected to understand enough of the response structure to know what a report meant when Weapons, Medical, Recon, or Security touched the same case. He did not need to perform their jobs. He needed enough context to know when the paper stopped matching the work.

The first lesson was already clear, even if the second was not: a wrong record did not stay on paper. Someone built a decision from it, and the decision built the next record. If he wanted to understand what was happening at East Beach, he would have to find the first place where the paper and the world stopped agreeing.

His private interface remained silent.

That was useful too.

It had not turned the correction into a reward. It had not produced a mission, a point, or a number.

The work was still his.
