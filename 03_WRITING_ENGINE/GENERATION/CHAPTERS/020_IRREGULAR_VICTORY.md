# IRREGULAR VICTORY

**Scene one â€” the exercise that was not supposed to be real.**

The field south of the Academy was marked into lanes by orange flags that leaned in the wind. Arthur stood at the edge with the rest of the exercise roster, waiting for an instructor with a clipboard to finish pretending they were not the last group to be briefed.

It was a graded inter-division exercise, not a mission. The teaching staff called it a "cooperative readiness test." The Combat students called it "babysitting the file clerks." The Logistics and Medical students stood in separate clusters, close enough to hear the same briefing but not so close that anyone would mistake them for a single team. Arthur watched the Combat students stand at the front of the briefing area, where the grass was level and the instructor's voice carried best. The support students stood in the back, near the supply crates, where the ground sloped and the flags were faded.

Arthur did not look at the Combat line. He looked at the Irregulars.

Owen stood with his notebook open, writing flicker-counts in a shorthand he had invented in the first week. Beside him, Clarke held a folder against his chest with both arms, as if it were a shield. Clarke's Ability was document-reading: he could read age, handling, and origin from paper and ink better than anyone in the annex. In direct combat, it was approximately as useful as a spoon.

"Reed," the instructor said.

Arthur stepped forward.

"You are attached as records support. Your team is the stamped-student cluster. The scenario is a mock anomaly in the east lane. You are to document, classify, and contain it in ninety minutes."

"Who leads?" Owen asked.

"Whoever the team follows."

The instructor moved on before he could explain what that meant.

**Scene two â€” the east lane.**

The mock anomaly was a painted circle on the ground with a sensor box in the center. It pulsed with a low tone that the briefing described as "anomalous resonance." The students who were not wearing Operations Support pins kept a distance from it. The Combat student who had briefed them â€” Kato, according to the name tape above his pocket â€” set the perimeter at ten meters and told the Logistics student to mark it with chalk.

"No one touches the box until the sensor is read," Kato said.

The Logistics student knelt at the edge of the circle, holding a measuring tape and a stick of chalk. The Medical student stood behind her with a hazard tag already half-written. They were waiting for someone with a Combat or Tactical pin to give the next order. No one looked at Arthur.

"We need a perimeter," Kato said. He was the same height as the formal lead from the waterfront, with the same habit of flattening his hands before he spoke. "Then we need someone to read the sensor."

"The sensor is wrong," Clarke said.

Everyone looked at him.

"It's a training box," Kato said.

"It is a training box," Clarke agreed. "But the label on it is from the east warehouse, and the tape on the corners is from the west calibration room. Someone assembled it from two different stores."

Kato opened his mouth, then closed it. He had not expected a clerk to notice something that fast.

"So what?" he said.

"So the scenario might be real," Owen said. His voice was quiet but steady. "The box is a training box, but the resonance tone is one point two hertz higher than the standard simulation frequency. I've been logging flickers for three weeks. This one is outside the training set."

"A flicker is not an anomaly," Kato said. "It's weather."

"Weather doesn't follow a box," Owen said.

He pointed past the painted circle. In the tall grass beyond the lane, something had left a trail wide enough for three people walking side by side. The grass was not trampled. It was bent in a single direction, smooth as a comb.

Arthur looked at the two of them. Owen with his flicker-log. Clarke with his document eye. Kato, who expected to be in charge. A Medical student checking her kit. A Logistics student measuring the lane. They were all waiting for the same thing: someone to tell them which of the four steps in the manual came next.

"What are the conditions?" Arthur asked.

"Containment," Kato said. "Standard. We cordon the circle and wait for the tone to stop."

"That is not the problem," Arthur said.

Kato turned.

"The problem is the box," Arthur said. "If the box was assembled from two stores, then the resonance is not the anomaly. The resonance is a side effect of the box being in the wrong place. The real anomaly is whatever moved the box."

"There is no anomaly in the briefing," Kato said.

"There is a real one now," Owen said.

"Flicker?" Arthur asked.

"Low-grade Seep," Owen said. "Object-class. It's been moving up and down the lane since before we arrived. The tone drew it."

Kato looked at the grass. He looked at Arthur.

"Then we cordon the trail, not the box."

"No," Arthur said. "We follow the trail to where it stops. The Seep follows the box, and the box follows the tone. We turn the tone off, and the Seep settles."

"How do you know it settles?" Kato asked. His voice had tightened.

"Because Owen's log says it has stopped twice in the last twenty minutes. Both times, it stopped where the tone was weakest."

Owen nodded, surprised that Arthur had noticed.

"The safest approach is to reduce the tone," the Medical student said.

"Exactly," Arthur said.

Kato looked at each of them in turn. The hierarchy in his face wavered for a moment. He was the one with the higher pin, the longer training record, the instructor's implicit blessing. But he was also the one standing in front of a problem none of his drills had covered.

"Show me," he said to Owen.

Owen held out the notebook. The page was a grid of times, frequencies, and small marks. Arthur watched Kato's eyes move across it. Kato was not reading it â€” he did not have the key â€” but he saw the pattern: regular entries, no guesses, no dramatic language.

"Thirty minutes," Kato said. "If the Seep moves before you finish, we cordon and wait for the real response team."

"It will not move," Owen said. "Not if we lower the tone."

"Then lower it."

Clarke knelt by the box. The Medical student stood beside him with a tag ready. Owen held his notebook in front of him like a compass. Arthur watched the grass and the tone and the distance, and he mapped the only thing that mattered: who had the right Ability for the next minute. If the Seep moved, Owen would read the flicker. If the box needed another adjustment, Clarke would read the wear. If the Seep reached them, Kato's Combat training would be the thing that mattered. Everyone had a place. None of them were the same.

**Scene three â€” the wrong log.**

They found the Seep at the end of the trail, where the grass gave way to a drainage ditch. It was an old survey marker, the kind that recorded land boundaries before the Academy was built. Clarke identified the year from the patina without touching it. Owen confirmed the flicker matched the tone. The Logistics student built a frame around it. The Medical student certified the site non-hazardous. Kato stood at the edge of the frame and did not look away until the tag was tied.

"That's it?" Kato asked.

"That's it," Owen said.

"It doesn't look like much."

"It isn't much," Clarke said. "That is why no one noticed it."

The Seep was contained without anyone touching it.

Back at the Academy, the instructor reviewed the logs. Arthur had written the method notes: flicker-log as sensor, document-reading as calibration-check, aptitude-map as positioning. The instructor read it twice.

"This is unusually complete," he said.

"It is a record of what happened," Arthur said.

"It reads like a plan," the instructor said. "Most records are written after the fact. This one reads as if it were written before it happened."

"It was written as it happened," Arthur said. "The plan was revised every minute."

The instructor turned the page. "The Combat team filed a separate report. They claim the exercise became an active mission when the Seep was identified."

"It did," Arthur said.

"But your report says the Seep was drawn by the box before the exercise began."

"Yes."

"Which means the box was already wrong when it was placed in the lane."

"Yes."

The instructor closed the log. "Someone in Logistics is going to be unhappy."

Arthur did not answer. He had seen Kato's face change when Clarke read the box, and again when Owen named the Seep. The look had not been friendly. It had been the look of someone who had sorted a thing one way and was now being asked to sort it another.

"Is there a problem?" the instructor asked.

"No," Arthur said.

He took the log back to the records block and filed it under the Irregular cohort's exercise ledger. Owen followed him. Clarke followed Owen.

"They are going to call it a fluke," Owen said. "Kato already did, in his report. He said the box was mislabeled, not that we did anything special."

"Let him," Arthur said.

"It is not a fluke," Clarke said. "The box was mislabeled. That is the point. Anyone could have read it."

"Anyone did not," Owen said. "We did."

"They are going to ask us to do it again," Owen said.

"Yes," Arthur said.

"And the next time, they will design the exercise so we fail."

Arthur looked at the ledger. "Then we will fail in a way that teaches them something."

Clarke smiled for the first time since the exercise began. It was a small, dry expression, but it was there.

"That is the least reassuring thing anyone has ever said to me," he said.

**Scene four â€” the flag.**

That evening, the notice board outside the dining hall carried a revised posting. The Irregular-classified students had moved from the bottom of the exercise points to the top of the current exercise standings. The margin next to their names was small, but it was there. The result fed their Academy Performance Record; it did not alter any of their Esper Ranks.

Arthur read it once. Then he walked to the bins and ate with the others.

Owen sat across from him. Clarke sat beside him. The girl with the moving hands tapped her cup. The boy with no insignia ate without speaking.

"They are looking at us now," Owen said.

"Because of the points?" Arthur asked.

"Because of the points, the board, and the fact that people remember who moves up."

"They were already looking," Arthur said.

"No. Before, they looked to sort us. Now they look because they are not sure where to put us."

Arthur thought about that. Kato's face. The instructor's reading. The small margin on the board.

"That is called irritation," Clarke said. "Next time it will be called rivalry."

"How do you know?" Owen asked.

"Because I read a lot of after-action reports. Irritation becomes rivalry the moment the other side admits you might win twice."

Arthur picked up his tray. The margin was small. The change was real.

He carried both back to the archive.
