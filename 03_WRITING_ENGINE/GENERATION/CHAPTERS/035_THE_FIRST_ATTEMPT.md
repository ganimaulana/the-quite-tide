# FIRST ATTEMPT

**Scene one â€” the plan.**

Arthur wrote the plan in the records block while the others watched. The Seep followed memory, so they needed to introduce a new memory stronger than the old one. The warehouse's front door was the current return point because every worker who had ever lost something had looked for it there.

"We move the witnesses," Arthur said.

"There are no witnesses," Dr. Kajiwara said. "The warehouse is closed."

"There is us. We are witnesses. If we all look at a new point, the Seep will follow the stronger memory."

"And then?"

"Then we frame it."

Owen looked nervous. Clarke looked interested. Pram, who had been included in the team after the fourth mission, looked at the plan for a while and then said, "How many witnesses is stronger than a hundred?"

"That is the experiment," Arthur said.

"That is not an answer."

"No. It is the reason for the experiment."

Pram accepted that. He had learned, over four missions, that Arthur's plans usually contained one unknown on purpose, and that the unknown was usually the part that mattered.

The plan was simple. They would stand in the warehouse for one hour, all of them, looking at a single point on the floor. They would not speak, not move, and not look away. By the end of the hour, their memory of the point would be the most recent and the most concentrated. If the Seep followed recent concentrated memory, the tools would move to the point. If it followed old diffuse memory, they would stay at the door.

"We get one hour," Arthur said. "If it fails, the Seep will know we tried, and the next attempt will be harder."

"What does a Seep know?" the Medical student asked.

"More than we do," Clarke said.

**Scene two â€” the failure.**

They stood in a circle around a chair in the center of the warehouse. Arthur told them to stare at the chair and think of it as the place where the missing things belonged.

The heat was off. Their breath showed. The Medical student shifted her weight twice, and each time Owen wrote it down, because movement was a variable.

For an hour, nothing happened.

Then the Seep moved everything on the floor toward the chair. Tools, gloves, a clipboard â€” they all slid across the concrete and stopped at the chair's legs.

"It worked," Owen said.

"No," Arthur said.

The objects stayed at the chair for three seconds. Then they went back to the front door.

"It remembered both places," Clarke said. "It did not choose the new one."

"Because the old memory has more witnesses," Arthur said. "Years of them. We are six people. The warehouse has had hundreds."

Pram picked up a wrench from the pile at the door and set it carefully back down. "So the new memory is weaker."

"Not weaker. Smaller. The Seep counts witnesses, not minutes."

"Then the count can be beaten."

"Only by a larger count. Which we do not have."

Pram looked at the door. "What if we make more witnesses?"

"Out of what?"

"Out of the record. You said the old case had a photograph. If the photograph is a witness, then the photograph counts."

Arthur looked at him. It was the same move Clarke made with documents, applied to a phenomenon, and it was the first time Pram had ever proposed a method instead of a complaint.

"It might," Arthur said. "It depends whether the Seep can read paper."

"That is the experiment," Pram said. "Your experiment had one unknown. Mine has one too."

Dr. Kajiwara wrote something down and underlined it. Arthur saw the underline and understood that Pram's contribution had just been recorded for the Institute, which meant Pram had just become part of the method, which meant the Institute would read his name next to Arthur's.

"Then we test it," Arthur said. "Photograph first. Then the old worker."

"Both tomorrow," Pram said. "The old man is not going to wait for a second experiment."

"How do you know there is an old man?"

"Because you already requested the 1978 register." Pram nodded at Arthur's folder. "You do not request a register unless you expect a name."

Dr. Kajiwara wrote something down. "Then the Seep is anchored by collective memory, not current intention."

Arthur nodded. "We need a witness who was there before."

"Where do we find one?"

"From the employment log. The warehouse closed in 2014, so the last workers are not old. But the district was built in 1927, and the ferry ran until 1974. There was a different warehouse here before this one."

He opened the cold-case file and put the 1927 photograph on the chair. The one with the ticket window and the pencil date on the back.

"The workers from 1927 are dead," he said. "But their record is not. If the Seep follows witnesses, then maybe it also follows the record of a witness."

"That is a bigger guess than the chair," Pram said.

"Yes. That is why we try it next."

**Scene three â€” the old worker.**

They did not get to try the 1927 record immediately. The next morning, the Logistics student brought in an employment register from the 1978 warehouse â€” the one that had stood on the site before the current building â€” and in it, one name had been crossed out with a note: *injury, retired, still local.*

They found him in a boarding house near the river. He was seventy, retired, and missing three fingers from an accident in the warehouse in 1978.

"I lost them in the door," he said. "Always thought they went back there."

Arthur asked him to come. The old man came because the Institute paid for his time and because he wanted to know where his fingers were.

At the warehouse, the old man walked to the front door and stood where he must have stood every working day of his life. He put his remaining fingers on the brass plate.

"Here," he said. "I was holding the door when the load shifted. I dropped them here."

Arthur waited. The Seep had to feel the memory. It had to recognize the old man's witness as the true one.

Nothing happened.

"It does not remember me," the old man said.

"Why not?" Owen asked.

"Because I was not looking at my fingers. I was looking at the man who came through the door." The old man turned to Arthur. "I lost them because someone came in. I never saw my fingers fall."

Arthur understood. The Seep returned things to the last place they were seen. The fingers were last seen by the man who came through the door.

"Who was he?" Arthur asked.

"A clerk. Young. Named Reed."

Arthur's hand went still.

**Scene four â€” the name again.**

The old man did not remember the clerk's face. He remembered the coat, the way the man held the door, and the fact that he had come in from the rain without shaking off his umbrella. Small things, but small things were what the Seep counted.

Arthur walked to the door and stood where the clerk had stood. He did not hold anything. He did not look for the fingers. He only stood, and the team watched him.

The Seep moved.

Not the tools. The dust. The dust on the floor drifted toward Arthur's feet, slow and evenly, as if the room had exhaled.

"It sees the clerk," Clarke said.

"It sees the coat," Arthur said. "Or the name."

He stepped back, and the dust stopped. He stepped forward, and it resumed. The Seep was not following him. It was following something the old man's memory had attached to the doorway. Something the clerk had left there in 1978, and something Arthur now carried.

He took the artifact out of his pocket. The dust moved toward it across the whole length of the floor, gathering at his feet like a crowd.

"The artifact was here," Arthur said. "In 1978. The clerk carried it. The Seep remembers it."

Dr. Kajiwara stopped writing. For the first time since the cafe, she looked genuinely uncertain.

"That means the Seep is not a phenomenon," she said. "It is a record of the artifact's passage."

"No," Arthur said. "It means the Seep is the artifact's memory. And the artifact has been in this district for a hundred years."

He put the artifact away. The dust settled. He had come to solve a Seep, and the Seep had solved something for him instead: the artifact had a history, and the history had witnesses, and one of them was standing in the room.

The old man was still at the door. He had watched the dust move, and he had not asked what it was, because men of his generation did not ask what dust was. He only looked at Arthur's pocket.

"My father had something like that," he said.

"What?"

"A stone. He kept it in a tin. He said it was for the house." The old man frowned. "He gave it to my brother, not me. My brother went north when the ferry closed."

"Where north?"

"Nobody knows. He stopped writing in 1975." The old man looked at the door. "That is when the district started closing. First the ferry, then the warehouse, then the family."

Arthur wrote it all down. A stone. A tin. A brother who went north. 1975, which was one year after the east storage block closed, which was one year after the dormant file was written.

The chain was not a chain of events. It was a chain of people, and the people had kept moving, and each move had left a smaller record than the last.
