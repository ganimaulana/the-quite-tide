# EVAL: RE-EXAMINATION

**Scene one â€” the second evaluation.**

The instrument that had returned ERROR/UNDEFINED the first time was being run again as a diagnostic repeat, not as the annual classification cycle. Arthur stood in front of it while a senior official from the evaluation board watched from behind glass. He was older than the Academy instructors, with a face that had stopped showing surprise, and he had come from the capital for this one appointment, which was itself a fact.

The room was the same room from the first week. The Academy could repeat a diagnostic measurement when the classification record contained an unresolved error; this repeat could document the discrepancy, but it could not manually change Arthur's Rank. Grey cover gone, instrument exposed, humming. The technician who operated it was a different technician, and this one was nervous, which meant he knew who was behind the glass.

The instrument hummed. The needle did not move. The screen showed ERROR/UNDEFINED again.

"Again," the senior official said, through the speaker.

They ran it three more times. Each time, the result was the same. On the third run, the technician changed the calibration and got the same result, which meant the error was not in the calibration. On the fourth run, he changed the sequence and got the same result, which meant the error was not in the sequence.

The senior official stepped into the room. He did not look at the instrument. He looked at Arthur.

"The instrument is not broken," he said. "The question is wrong."

Arthur waited.

"We are asking what you are. We should be asking what you are not."

"What am I not?" Arthur asked.

"That is the question." The senior official turned to the instructor. "File the result as unresolved. Rank F. Re-examination at the next annual classification cycle."

The instructor wrote it down. Arthur's filing stayed F. The room emptied.

Before he left, the senior official stopped at the door and asked one more question, not to Arthur but to the technician.

"How many times has this instrument returned ERROR on a living subject?"

"Eleven," the technician said.

"And how many of those subjects were later re-classified?"

"One."

The official nodded, as if he had expected the number. Then he looked at Arthur for a second longer than necessary, and left.

Arthur wrote the exchange down that evening, because it was the first time anyone had given him a number instead of a verdict. Eleven. One. Ten people had stayed unresolved for their whole lives, and no one had written down what happened to them. That was the part of the record that was missing, and a missing part was where the useful question lived.

**Scene two â€” the wrong question.**

Hayes met him outside the evaluation hall. The corridor was cold, and their breath showed.

"What did he say?" Hayes asked.

"That the question is wrong."

"What question?"

"What I am."

Hayes's expression did not change, but his posture did. "That is a strong reading."

"He did not say what I am."

"He did not need to. He only needed to say the question is wrong." Hayes looked back at the hall. "A senior official does not watch a second evaluation unless he expects to see something."

"He saw ERROR/UNDEFINED."

"He saw it twice. Twice is a pattern." Hayes put a hand on Arthur's shoulder, then removed it, as if the gesture surprised him. "Be careful what you let them ask you next."

"What will they ask?"

"They will eventually ask whether you should be re-sorted. That is a separate administrative question. It cannot manually change your Esper Rank."

Arthur did not answer. He had been asked to re-sort twice already: once by the Institute, once by the Combat section. Both times he had deferred.

"There is a third possibility," Hayes said.

"What?"

"That the question is wrong because the answer is not a category." Hayes's voice was flat, the way it was when he was saying something he did not want to be quoted on. "The instrument reads categories. If a person is not a category, the instrument does not read them as low. It reads them as nothing."

"Is that what the official meant?"

"I don't know what the official meant. I know what he filed." Hayes turned to go. "Unresolved is not a low reading. It is the absence of a reading. Find out whether that is a gift or a sentence."

He walked away. Arthur stood in the corridor for a while, watching the breath leave his own mouth, and thought about absence.

Absence had a shape. He had seen it once before, in the annex: a field in a form that had been left blank because the clerk had not known what to write. The blank field had caused more problems than a wrong answer would have. A wrong answer could be corrected. A blank had to be investigated.

He was the blank. That was the thing Hayes had told him without saying it. Not a low value â€” a value that had never been entered. The Academy could not compare him to anything, because the instrument had refused to produce a comparison. Rank F was not a measurement. It was the form's way of saying *see attached*, and there was nothing attached.

---

**Scene three â€” filing stays F.**

Arthur filed the evaluation in his own cabinet, not the Academy's. The official form said Rank F. The margin said unresolved. The difference between the two was where the power sat.

He wrote a note under the result: **The instrument reads ERROR. The official reads unresolved. The filing reads F. All three are true if none of them are complete.**

Then he did what he always did with an unresolved thing: he cross-indexed it. He entered the evaluation under three headings. Under CATEGORY, he wrote Rank F. Under INSTRUMENT, he wrote ERROR/UNDEFINED, two occurrences. Under OFFICIAL READING, he wrote: *question is wrong; absence is not low.*

He did not know which heading would matter later. That was the point of cross-indexing. A fact filed under one heading could only be found one way. A fact filed under three could be found three ways, and the Academy's clerks were trained to search by heading.

Rieko had taught him that in the first week. She had said it without explaining it: *a clerk who files by the obvious heading is a clerk who can be bypassed.* He had not understood her then. He understood her now. Filing was not storage. Filing was access. The person who chose the heading chose who could find the thing.

He chose the headings carefully. CATEGORY, because the board would search that way. INSTRUMENT, because the technicians would. OFFICIAL READING, because the capital would. Three doors into one room.

---

**Scene four â€” the third flicker.**

That night, the Quiet Desk logged a third signal flicker. Arthur did not know it existed. He knew only that the senior official's eyes had been on him, and that the official had not looked away, and that a man from the capital does not travel for an appointment he expects to be routine.

He sat in the archive and read the evaluation form again. Rank F. Unresolved. Next annual classification cycle.

He thought about the annual classification rule. A supervising faculty member could document a discrepancy and request attention, but the official Rank could only change through the authorized annual classification cycle. The diagnostic repeat had proved that the instrument still returned the same unresolved result.

Outside, the campus was dark. The instrument hummed in its room under a fresh grey cover, and the needle sat exactly where it had sat the first time: between two categories that did not exist.

Arthur closed the cabinet. He had come to the Academy to learn why the instrument hesitated. Three months later, he had learned that it did not hesitate. It refused. And a refusal, he was beginning to understand, was the most useful record of all â€” because it told you exactly where the edge of the map was.

He went to bed. In the morning, he would request the 1974 files, and be refused, and file the refusal under OFFICIAL READING, third heading, first entry.

Before he slept, he lay in the dark and did the arithmetic one more time. Eleven subjects had returned ERROR. One had been re-classified. That meant ten had not. The instrument had been in service for thirty years. If a subject was assessed once a year, and ten of them had never been re-classified, then the system had carried ten unresolved people for three decades without a form for them.

He was not the first. He was the eleventh. And the first one had been re-classified â€” which meant there was a record of how it had been done, somewhere, in a drawer that nobody had opened in a long time.

That was a lead. He filed it in his head under a heading the Academy did not have: **THE TEN.**

Then he slept, and in the morning the request went out, and the refusal came back by noon, and he filed it as planned.

---

**Scene five — the cost of remaining unresolved.**

The next afternoon, Arthur received a notice from the Academy registry.

The diagnostic repeat had been entered into his student record.

The notice contained three lines.

**RANK: F.**

**CLASSIFICATION: IRREGULAR.**

**REVIEW STATUS: UNRESOLVED.**

Arthur read it once and felt the old irritation return.

Not anger. Not fear.

Administrative irritation.

The machine had failed to classify him, and the Academy had solved that failure by giving the failure a place to live.

He took the notice to Hayes.

"They have made the unresolved result permanent until the next annual cycle," Arthur said.

Hayes read it. "That is what the rules allow."

"I know."

"Then why are you here?"

Arthur tapped the third line. "Because this is the first time they have called it a review status instead of a provisional classification."

Hayes looked at him for several seconds. "You think the wording matters."

"Wording is how institutions move without admitting they moved."

Hayes almost smiled. "You have been spending too much time in Records."

"Probably."

Hayes returned the paper. "Then use it correctly. Do not turn every change in wording into a conspiracy."

Arthur nodded. "What should I turn it into?"

"A question with a measurable answer."

Arthur thought about the eleven subjects.

"Then I want the previous ten cases."

"You already requested the old files."

"No. I requested the 1974 files. I want the ten ERROR/UNDEFINED cases. All years."

Hayes's expression changed slightly. "That is a better request."

"Why?"

"Because now you are not asking where the first case went. You are asking whether the Academy has been handling the same problem repeatedly."

Arthur took out his notebook.

He wrote the request before he could talk himself out of it.

Ten cases.

Ten outcomes.

One reclassification.

Nine unresolved.

If the numbers held, the pattern would be more useful than the mystery.

If they did not, he would have learned that his first theory was wrong.

Either result was useful.

He signed the request and handed it to Hayes.

This time, Hayes did not tell him to wait.

He only said, "Good. Now we can find out what unresolved actually costs."
