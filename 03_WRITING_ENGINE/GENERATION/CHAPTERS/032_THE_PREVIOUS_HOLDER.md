The last day of the term was a Tuesday. Arthur filed the gauge audit, the deferred offer, and the cold-case note in the same folder, then put the folder in the bottom drawer of his desk because the things at the bottom were the things he was not yet ready to look at.

The residential block emptied in waves. First the Combat students, who had the earliest trains. Then the Tactical wing. Then the support divisions, who stayed an extra day to close their work areas because the Academy's holiday policy assumed that support students had nowhere to be.

The Academy emptied slowly. Students carried bags to the train station. Instructors locked offices. The records block stayed open because records did not take holidays. Margaret worked through the afternoon without looking up, and the boiler in the east wing was still off, and the whole building sat in the cold like a thing that had been left behind on purpose.

Arthur worked beside her. The low pile did not shrink for the holidays. If anything, it grew, because the term-end reports all arrived at once and all of them were misfiled by students who had written them in a hurry.

At four o'clock, Margaret stood up and put on her coat.

"I am going home," she said.

"You never go home this early."

"I am going home early because it is the holiday, and because there is nothing here that cannot wait until January." She looked at the drawer where Arthur had put the Reed folder. "Nothing except the thing you are not telling me about."

Arthur did not answer.

"Good," Margaret said. "Keep it that way. I sign forms for a living. I do not want to sign a form about you."

She left. The records block was quiet. Arthur finished the pile, closed the ledger, and locked the drawer.

Margaret handed him a new docket.

"This came in before the break. It was routed to the wrong pile."

Arthur read the cover sheet. It was a transfer request from an institute archive, asking for the cold-case ferry terminal file. The request had been denied twice before reaching him.

"Who wants it?" he asked.

"The same people who want you," Margaret said. "They ask for files you have touched."

Arthur read the request a second time. The wording was careful: *for purposes of comparative research.* No names. No deadline. No mention of the artifact. It was the politest possible way to say that someone had been reading his work.

He put the request in his pocket. He would answer it after the break.

**Scene two â€” the previous holder file.**

The previous holder's method was all he had. He went back to the cold-case docket and read every page that was not sealed. Most of it was routine. The sealed pages were marked by date: 1927, 1934, 1962. Each seal was older than the last, and each seal used a different stamp, which meant the rules for sealing had changed three times while the file sat in the drawer.

On the third page of the 1934 section, he found a name: **Reed**. Not a first name. Just the family name, written in the margin by a clerk who had not known how to file it.

Arthur stared at it. The warm stone in his pocket seemed heavier.

Reed was his own name. It might be a coincidence. The Academy had thousands of records. A shared family name meant nothing by itself. His own family had never mentioned the Academy, or an artifact, or a Seep. His mother had filed forms at a shipping office. His father had repaired instruments. Neither of them had ever said the word *holder*.

But the margin note was in the same hand as the other 1934 note, the one that described the method. And the method was the one the artifact had taught him.

He copied the page and put the original back. Then he wrote a note: **Previous holder: possible family connection. Status: unverified.**

He underlined *unverified* twice. Unverified was the only honest heading. A name was not a relative. A margin was not a memory.

Before he closed the docket, he did something he had not done with the other files. He photographed the margin with the annex camera â€” a small device Margaret used for damaged paper â€” and saved the image to his own locker, not the annex archive. He had begun, without announcing it, to keep a parallel record. The Academy's files could be read, moved, or sealed. A record in his own locker could only be read by him.

It was the first thing he had ever kept from the Academy. He noted the date and the reason in his notebook: *Parallel file opened, 16 December. Reason: three seals, three stamps, three hands. The file is being managed.*

A file that was being managed was a file someone cared about. Cared-about files were either treasures or traps, and the only way to find out which was to read them without permission.

**Scene three â€” the institute's first letter.**

The letter was waiting in his locker. It was not the same offer as before. It was a request for a meeting, informal, with a researcher named **Dr. Kajiwara**. The letter mentioned the ferry terminal file and the Reed note.

Arthur read it twice. The Institute was not asking to study him. It was asking to tell him something.

He checked the letter for the details he had learned to check. The seal was the Institute's, not the Academy's. The paper was Institute stock. The phrasing was personal, which meant it was written by one person and not by a committee. And the researcher's name appeared on the letter twice, which meant she wanted him to know who to ask for.

He put the letter with the offer and the docket. All three were open. All three were waiting.

He read the letter one more time before he put it away, and noticed the one detail he had missed. The meeting was proposed for a cafÃ© by the river, not at the Institute, and not at the Academy. Dr. Kajiwara had chosen neutral ground. That told him three things: she did not want the Institute to know, she did not want the Academy to know, and she expected him to arrive alone.

He wrote the three things down. Then he wrote a fourth: *She is either a friend or a recruiter. Both are useful to meet.*

He did not decide. He closed the drawer and stayed in the Academy residence for the break.

The residence was quieter than usual. Arthur sat by the window and watched the Academy grounds disappear into evening. In his pocket, the artifact was warm. In his folder, the Reed note was copied. In his bottom drawer, the offer was deferred.

He had more questions than he had started the break with. That was the term's only clear result.

**Scene four — the family archive.**

The family archive had been released to him in part after the investigation closed its first stage. It was not much: scanned photographs, school papers, a few household documents, and the surviving boxes that had not been damaged in the massacre.

He opened one box on the table beside the residence window.

The artifact was warm in his hand.

He remembered his mother keeping old papers in careful order. He remembered his father complaining whenever a drawer jammed. He remembered his sister leaving pens without their caps.

Those memories were clear.

The people were not coming back.

Arthur looked at the box for a long time before continuing.

Near the bottom was an old appraisal from 1987. The stones had been dismissed as *useless*.

He had heard that story from his family many times.

Now he had no one left to ask what the missing line meant.

He copied the document and placed the original back.
