# THE REVIEW THAT SEES

**Scene one â€” the reconciled log.**

The review board sat at a long table with both reports in front of them. Arthur stood at the back with Hayes. Hosoda stood at the other end, alone, his hands behind his back.

The board room was the same room where Arthur had been assessed in the first week. He remembered the instrument in the corner, the one that had hesitated for three beats. It was still there, under a grey cover, humming to itself.

"The Combat report says you ordered the team around the east side," the senior board member said.

"The Records report says the same thing," Hosoda said. "It says I ordered it."

"But you did not."

Hosoda's jaw tightened. "I did not."

"Then why sign it?"

"Because the building stopped. Because the method worked. Because the only version of the report that makes the Academy look competent is the one where someone led."

The board member looked at Arthur. "And you?"

"I observed."

"Your report reads like a command log."

"I described what each person did."

"Including yourself."

"Including myself."

The board turned pages. The reconciled version described Arthur's method accurately: the steady sound, the east-side anchor, the frame. It did not describe him as a leader. It described him as the source of the analysis. The senior member read the final paragraph aloud, because reading aloud was the board's way of making a thing official.

"'The containment succeeded because the observer identified the adaptive behavior of the phenomenon before the mission lead did, and proposed a method that the team adopted.'" He set the page down. "Is that accurate?"

"It is accurate," Arthur said.

"It omits that the mission lead rejected the method twice before adopting it."

"Yes."

"Why did you omit that?"

"Because the mission succeeded," Arthur said. "The report is about the mission."

Hayes, behind him, made a sound that was not quite a cough. The board member looked at the omission for a long moment, then wrote something in the margin.

That was worse, in its way. The report had protected Hosoda, and the board had noticed the protection, and now the protection itself was in the record. Arthur had tried to file a fact cleanly, and the board had filed the filing.

The junior board member, who had said nothing until then, asked the question Arthur had been waiting for.

"Where did you learn to read a building?"

"The annex," Arthur said. "A Seep in the south wing. It returned sound to the last place it had accepted."

"Was that a Contract matter?"

The room went still. Arthur looked at the junior member, then at Hayes, then at the cover over the instrument.

"It was a filing matter," he said.

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer I have that is complete."

The senior member wrote another line in the margin and closed the folder. The hearing ended. As Arthur left, he felt the instrument under its grey cover turn toward him â€” or he imagined it, which was its own kind of information. He did not look back to check.

**Scene two â€” Hayes's crack.**

After the review, Hayes stopped him in the corridor.

"They will keep two reports now," Hayes said. "The official one, and the one they write about the official one."

"That was already true."

"It is now true about you." Hayes's voice was the same professional dismissal as always, but there was something underneath it that had not been there before. Not respect. Watchfulness. "The board sees a pattern. Patterns get filed."

"They filed the method."

"They filed your name next to the method. That is not the same thing."

Arthur looked at him. Hayes was not going to say more. The crack in his contempt was small, but it was there, and it had a shape: it was the shape of a man who had decided that the clerk was worth warning.

"Thank you," Arthur said.

"I did not say it as advice. I said it as warning."

"Same shape."

Hayes almost smiled. "Different result."

He walked away before Arthur could answer. In the three months since the assessment, Hayes had dismissed him, ignored him, signed his forms, and now warned him. Arthur wrote the sequence down in his notebook because sequences were what he understood. The sequence was: contempt, contempt, notice, warning. The next entry in the sequence was not yet written, and that was the part he could not predict.

There was a fourth thing in the sequence, though he did not write it down. Hayes had said "as a person," and then corrected himself, as if the phrase had escaped without permission. Arthur had noticed it because noticing was his job, and because the phrase was the only part of the conversation that Hayes had not meant to file. It meant that under the advisor's contempt there was something that had an opinion about him. That was not the same as respect. It was closer to a decision that had not yet been made.

**Scene three â€” flicker number two.**

That evening, the Quiet Desk received its second signal flicker from the Academy. The first had come after the over-invocation in the south annex, in the week when the mark had appeared on his hand. The second came after the waterfront review.

Arthur did not know the desk existed. He did not know the flicker had been logged. He knew only that his name had been filed twice in a week as the reason a strange thing made sense. The first filing had been an anomaly. The second filing was a pattern.

He sat in the archive and wrote the time of the review, the names of the board members, and the difference between the two reports. Then he wrote, in smaller letters, the only thing he was certain of: the Seep had been contained. The rest was paper.

Outside the window, the campus lights were coming on in rows. The Combat hall, the Tactical wing, the Medical annex, one after another, until the whole north side of the Academy was lit and the records block was the only dark building on the ridge. Arthur looked at it for a while. Then he turned back to the ledger and finished the entry.

He wrote the entry the way he wrote everything: what happened, who was present, what was said, and what could not be verified. Under **unverified**, he wrote three items. The first was whether the junior board member already knew about the annex. The second was whether the instrument had moved. The third was whether Hayes's warning had been a warning about the board or a warning about himself.

He did not answer any of the three. Answers were for the record, and the record did not owe him one.

**Scene four â€” the reputation.**

By the end of the week, the reconciled review had been read by every division. Reputation was a strange machine: it did not move by what a person did. It moved by what other people were willing to repeat. Arthur had not told anyone about the waterfront mission. He had not needed to. The board had written the sentence, and the sentence had traveled.

In the dining hall, two Tactical students nodded at him. In the corridor, a Medical student asked him whether the adaptive-behavior method was written down anywhere. In the training hall, a Combat student whose name he did not know said, loudly enough to be heard by his friends, that the clerk had "gotten lucky with a building."

Arthur answered each one the same way: with the plainest true thing, and nothing more. He did not correct the ones who said he was lucky. He did not accept the ones who said he was brilliant. Both were guesses, and guesses about him were not his to manage.

Owen found him in the records block that night.

"You are becoming known," Owen said.

"I am becoming filed."

"Is that different?"

"Yes. Known means they think they understand you. Filed means they have stopped trying." Arthur closed the ledger. "I prefer filed."

Owen looked at him with the flicker-notebook open in his hands. "That is the strangest thing anyone has said to me," he said, "and I write down strange things for a living."

Arthur did not answer. He put the ledger on the shelf and went to dinner.

But later, walking back through the dark between the records block and the residential block, he thought about what Owen had said. Being filed was not the same as being safe. A file could be read by anyone. A file could be copied, moved, misfiled on purpose. He had spent three months learning that the Academy's real currency was not rank but records, and records could be rewritten by whoever held the pen.

He stopped at the edge of the quadrangle. Somewhere across the campus, the instrument sat under its cover, humming. Somewhere above it, a desk he had never seen was logging a second flicker in his name. He could not see either of them. He could only file the fact that they existed.

That was enough for tonight. He went to bed.
