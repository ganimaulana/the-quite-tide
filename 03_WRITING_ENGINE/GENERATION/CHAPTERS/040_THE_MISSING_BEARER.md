**Scene one â€” the genealogy room.**

The Academy kept a genealogy room in the east wing, mostly for students who wanted to trace Attuned bloodlines. Arthur went there because the Reed notes were not a bloodline, but they were a line.

The room smelled of dust and leather. The shelves ran to the ceiling, and the archivist worked at a desk by the window with a lamp and a magnifier.

The archivist was a thin woman with reading glasses on a chain. "Reed?" she said. "Common name."

"I need a specific Reed. Or all of them, if they are few enough."

She laughed. It was the first time anyone had laughed at him in weeks.

"There are records going back three hundred years. You want all of them?"

"I want the ones associated with Seep investigations, ferry terminals, or lost artifacts."

The smile faded. "That is specific."

"I have a thread. I am following it."

She brought him six boxes.

The archivist watched him work for a while, then said, "You are sorting by hand, not by shelf."

"The shelf order is alphabetical. The pattern is chronological."

"That is slower."

"It is more honest. Alphabetical hides the gaps." Arthur laid two death records side by side. "A shelf tells you what exists. A chart tells you what does not."

The archivist considered that. "You are the first student to use this room for a chart. Most of them use it to find a famous ancestor."

"I am not looking for an ancestor."

"Then what are you looking for?"

"A gap," Arthur said. "A missing person leaves a hole in a family record, and a hole has edges. I want the edges."

She did not laugh at that. She went back to her desk and left him to the boxes.

**Scene two â€” the pattern.**

The boxes contained birth records, death records, and transfer slips. Arthur sorted them by date and place. He worked for four hours, and by the end he had a chart.

The chart was the first thing he had made that was not a copy. Twelve names, three hundred years, five Seep records, two artifact bearers, one missing gap. He drew it on a wide sheet of paper, with the dates across the top and the names down the side, and when he was finished it looked like a family tree that had been trimmed in the middle.

He held it up. The gap between 1934 and 1984 was the widest part of the chart, and it sat exactly where the artifact line should have continued. Everything before it pointed forward. Everything after it pointed back. The gap was not an absence. It was a knot.

Twelve Reed names across three hundred years. Five had Seep records. Three had been clerks. Two had been listed as "artifact bearer" in Institute documents. One had gone north in 1975 and never written again.

The artifact bearers were separated by generations. One died in 1934. The other in 1984.

The gap was fifty years.

Arthur looked at the dates. If the artifact passed from bearer to bearer, there should have been someone between 1934 and 1984. There was no record of that person.

"Missing bearer," he wrote. "1934â€“1984."

The artifact was silent in his pocket.

He checked the chart again. The 1934 bearer had died in the warehouse district. The 1984 bearer had died in the north. Between them, nothing. No birth, no death, no transfer. Fifty years of silence in a family that had otherwise left a record every generation.

He asked the archivist for the county death register for the intervening years. She brought it. He read it for two hours. There was no Reed death in the district between 1934 and 1984, and no Reed birth either, which meant the line had not produced a child in fifty years.

That was impossible for a family. It was possible for a position.

**Scene three â€” the missing bearer.**

The missing bearer was not missing. The missing bearer was the clerk.

Arthur laid the handwriting copies side by side: 1927, rightward hook. 1934, leftward hook. 1975, rightward hook. 1978, the same hand as 1927. Four dates, two hands, one name, and one person who had been present at each.

If the clerk of 1927 was the same clerk of 1978, then he had been working for fifty-one years, which was possible if he had started at eighteen and never retired. The two hands could be explained by age: a writer's hand changed over decades, and a young man's hook could become an old man's hook.

But the 1934 hook was the wrong direction. A hand did not reverse its hook in seven years and then reverse back in forty-one.

So there were two people. One who wrote rightward, and one who wrote leftward, and they shared a name because the name was not a person's name. It was the position's name, assigned at the start of each tenure, the way a ship's name stays on a ship.

Arthur wrote the conclusion carefully: **The bearer line is not a bloodline. It is an office. The office is named Reed. The holders may be unrelated.**

Then he wrote the harder line, the one he did not want to write: **If the office is named Reed, and I am named Reed, then I may be in the line by assignment, not by birth.**

He sat with that for a long time. The archivist closed the room at five and did not ask him what he had found.

But before she left, she stopped at the door and said, "The Reed who went north in 1975. There is a postal record for that family. It was donated to the wing by someone, years ago. No one has ever asked for it."

Arthur looked up. "Who donated it?"

"The card says *anonymous*." She shrugged. "But anonymous donations are logged by the archivist who receives them, and that archivist retired in 1996, and her log is in the third box you already opened."

She left. Arthur opened the third box again. At the bottom was a receipt, yellowed with age, for a donation of family papers dated 1975. The receiving archivist's name was legible. Beside it, the donor had signed with two hands: a rightward hook and a leftward correction.

The same two hands. Donated in 1975, the same year the brother went north.

The file was not a file. It was a dining hallage, mailed decades ago, and Arthur had just found the postmark.

**Scene four â€” the offer increases.**

The Institute's new offer arrived by courier. This one named a stipend, a rank, and a private archive. It also named a deadline: end of term.

Arthur put it with the other offer. He did not sign.

He took out the chart he had made and added a line at the bottom: **If the office is real, there is a record of the assignment. If there is a record of the assignment, there is a person who made it.**

The assignment of a position required an appointing authority. Someone had named the first Reed. Someone had continued the line. That someone was not the keeper. That someone was outside the chain, and outside the chain was where the answer lived.

He wrote in his notebook: **The missing bearer is the one who matters.**

Then he went to the dining hall and ate with the Irregulars. Clarke noticed the chart under his arm and asked about it. Arthur told him the truth but not all of it: a family name, a line of clerks, a gap in the record.

"That is a genealogy problem," Clarke said.

"It is a filing problem," Arthur said. "The record is missing a person. A missing person leaves a shape."

"What shape?"

"The shape of whoever filled the gap." Arthur put the chart away. "Someone kept the office running for fifty years without a holder. That is not a family. That is an institution."

Clarke looked at him for a moment. "You are describing the Institute."

"No," Arthur said. "I am describing something older than the Institute. The Institute is trying to buy it. You do not buy something you already own."

He finished his dinner. In the morning, he would request the Institute's founding charter, which was a public document, which meant he could read it without asking anyone's permission. He wanted to know what year the Institute had started, and whether that year fell inside the missing-bearer gap.

He also wanted to know who had signed it. A founding charter had founders. Founders had names. And if one of the founders had signed with two hands, then the two hands were not a clerk's habit. They were a signature, and the signature belonged to whoever had kept the office running while it had no holder.

That was the missing bearer. Not a person in the gap. A person above it.

He went to bed with the chart on his desk and the receipt under his pillow, which was not where a clerk kept a document, but was where a person kept the thing he was not willing to lose.
