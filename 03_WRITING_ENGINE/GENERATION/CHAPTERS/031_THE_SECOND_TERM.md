# END-I: SLOT 2 AND THE HOOK

**Scene one â€” the gauge audit.**

The term-end audit was held in a room with a long table and a machine that hummed. Arthur stood in front of it while two instructors checked readings from a clipboard. The artifact was in its pouch, warm against his leg.

The machine was not the same instrument that had shown ERROR/UNDEFINED. This one measured the artifact itself: its temperature, its mass, the resonance of its surface. It had been brought up from the capital in a padded case, and the instructor who operated it wore gloves.

"Gauge reading," one instructor said.

The other wrote a number. "Current Depth: within operational range. Lifetime Depth: sufficient for scheduled review."

"Capacity status?"

"Secondary capacity marker: available."

They did not say a Contract slot had unlocked. They said a secondary capacity marker was available, which was the Academy's way of recording a physical change without claiming to understand its mechanism.

Arthur felt the artifact shift slightly in the pouch. He did not take it out. Whatever had changed, the capacity marker was real.

"Sign here," the instructor said.

Arthur signed. The form noted that The secondary capacity marker remained unused. Capacity, not acquisition. The Academy understood the difference.

"One note," the second instructor said, without looking up. "The gauge records availability, not mechanism. We do not know what caused the marker to change. We only know that it is available."

"Understood," Arthur said.

"Most students ask why."

"I know why."

Both instructors looked up. Arthur did not explain. The form had a field for *student remarks*, and he left it blank. Some records were stronger without a remark.

But after he signed, the older instructor walked him to the door and spoke quietly, out of the machine's hearing.

"The first marker changed in September," she said. "The second became available in December. The gauge calls that a pattern. The capital calls it an anomaly. I am telling you because you will read the form anyway, and I would rather you heard it from a person."

"Thank you," Arthur said.

"Do not thank me. I am required to tell you. It is on the back of the form, in small print." She opened the door. "Most students do not read the back."

Arthur read the back of the form that night. The small print said: *Capacity-marker availability does not establish causation. Student is advised that the academy does not interpret gauge patterns without committee review.* Which meant, in plain language, that they had noticed the pattern and had decided not to look at it yet.

He filed the back of the form with the front. It was the same page. They had just printed it in two directions.

**Scene two â€” the aggregate.**

The term's Joint Team after-action aggregate was posted outside the records block. Arthur read it once.

The stamped-student cohort had the highest inclusion-request rate in Intelligence and Research. Not the highest score. Not the highest rank. The highest request rate. Other divisions were asking for them before missions. That was the metric that mattered, because a request was a decision made in advance, by someone who expected to need them.

"They still do not know what to do with us," Owen said.

"They know what to do with us," Arthur said. "They do not know what to call us."

Clarke pointed to the bottom of the aggregate. "Combat has filed a request to limit Irregular inclusion to one per mission."

"That is a procedural retaliation," Arthur said. "It means we are being budgeted."

"That is good?"

"That is real."

The aggregate also showed something Arthur had not expected. Under *subjects of inquiry*, the Irregular cohort now appeared in four different division reports, not as personnel but as a method. Three divisions had cited the appendix. One had cited the disputed command assessment. The Academy was not asking for him. It was asking for the thing he had written, and the thing he had written was now in the mouths of instructors who had never met him.

He read that line twice, then closed the board and went to lunch. The method had escaped the annex. He had not decided whether that was the plan or the problem.

At lunch, Clarke put the question plainly.

"Are you pleased?"

"No."

"Are you worried?"

"No."

"Then what are you?"

Arthur thought about it. The method was in four reports. The reports had been written by instructors who had never met him. The method would be used by students he would never meet. It would work, or it would fail, and either way his name was in the footnote.

"I am filed," he said.

Clarke nodded, as if that were the answer he had expected. "Then you have what you wanted in September."

"In September I wanted to be left alone."

"And now?"

"Now I want to be read carefully." Arthur finished his tea. "There is a difference, and it took me a term to learn it."

**Scene three â€” one page.**

The cold-case file was on Arthur's desk when he returned. Someone in the east wing had left it out for him. It was the same Drowning-year case from the ferry terminal, the one he had copied in November.

This time, one page was open. The page contained a name, a date, and a method. The name was not a person. It was a role: **Mooring-keeper, 1927**. The method was the same one he had seen before: keep the intake record and wait for the return to match.

Arthur read the page twice. Then he read it a third time.

The previous holder was not a person. It was a position. The artifact had been kept before. The keeping had cost the keeper something, but the page did not say what. There was a line at the bottom that had been partly erased, and what remained read: *...and the keeper shall not* â€” and then nothing.

He looked at the erased line for a long time.

He closed the file. The previous-holder thread was open. The dormant file was open. The artifact's nature was open.

Three threads at the end of one term.

He wrote the page number in his notebook and put the file back where he had found it, because taking it would have told whoever left it out that he had understood what they were offering.

**Scene four â€” the open offer.**

That night, he took the Institute's offer from its envelope. The deadline was the last day of term. The response he had sent had been answered: the terms were unchanged. They would wait until he decided.

Arthur did not decide. He put the offer beside the artifact and the cold-case file and the gauge audit. Three open items. One unused capacity marker. One mark on his hand. One failure survived.

He took out a blank card and wrote, in the format of an annex custody entry:

*Item: personal record, Arc I. Keeper: Arthur Reed, Rank F. Location: Academy. Status: open.*

Then he wrote the summary he would keep in his own cabinet, the one that no division would ever read:

**Term ends. Rank F. One Contract. One mark. One failure survived. One file nobody should have opened.**

He looked at the four lines. They were short. They were accurate. Then he added the fifth line, because Clarke had taught him that the true record was the one that included the part you did not want to write:

**Five people now know the method. One of them is not my friend.**

He counted them while the ink dried. Clarke knew, because he had helped write it. Owen knew, because the flicker-log was half the method. Hayes knew, because he had read both reports. The auditor knew, because he had demanded the proof. And Pram knew, because he had been the boundary.

Five. Of the five, one was not a friend. Clarke was a friend. Owen was a friend. Hayes was not a friend, but he was not hostile, and Hayes had never pretended otherwise. The auditor was neutral. Pram was â€” Arthur wrote the word carefully â€” *unaligned*. Unaligned was more dangerous than hostile, because a hostile person could be predicted, and an unaligned person could be anything.

He closed the notebook. Outside, the campus was quiet, and beyond it the city was quiet, and beyond the city was a border that no student had ever crossed. Somewhere past that border, the dormant source was still filed wrong, and the previous holder's line was still unbroken, and a man from the capital was still deciding what to do about the eleventh ERROR.

Arthur went to sleep. Arc I was over. In the morning, the second term would begin. Whatever waited there, he had already decided he would not accept it blindly. He would not form another Contract until he understood what the first one had cost.

**Scene five — the question he could not file.**

Arthur returned to the records block before the holiday closed it. He found Margaret at the counter, sorting requests into three piles: routine, delayed, and dangerous.

She looked at the paper in his hand. “Which pile?”

“I do not know yet.”

“Then it is the third.”

Arthur put the family appraisal beside the ferry docket. “The name Reed appears in the old records.”

Margaret did not reach for the paper. “And?”

“I do not know whether it means anything.”

“Good.”

“Good?”

“A clerk who does not know is safer than a clerk who decides too early,” Margaret said. “If you find a Reed, write *Reed*. Do not write *my ancestor* because you want the file to become interesting.”

The correction was irritating because it was right.

Arthur crossed out *possible* in his note and replaced it with *unverified*. Then he added the source beside it: 1934 margin note. No corroboration.

Margaret finally glanced at the page. “That is better.”

“You did not even read it.”

“I read the part that matters.” She tapped the correction. “You are learning.”

Arthur put the paper away. He had spent months trying to make records tell him what they meant. Now he was beginning to understand that records did not owe him a conclusion.

They only owed him what had happened.

That was less satisfying.

It was also more useful.

He closed the drawer and checked the lock twice. The holiday could begin—not because the questions were finished, but because for the first time he had a method for carrying them without turning them into answers.
