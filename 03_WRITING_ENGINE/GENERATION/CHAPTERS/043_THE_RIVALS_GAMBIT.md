# UEDA'S GAMBIT

**Scene one â€” the challenge.**

Mercer posted the challenge on the notice board outside the dining hall. It was a formal request for a head-to-head exercise, D-rank versus F-rank, phenomenon to be selected by lot. The winner would earn the right to be listed first on the next Joint Team roster.

Arthur read it while the dining hall emptied around him.

"He wants a public win," Owen said.

"He wants a public test," Clarke said. "If he wins, he proves rank matters. If he loses, he proves you are an anomaly. Either way, he gets information."

"Then I will give him a result he cannot use," Arthur said.

The challenge was drafted carefully. It named the terms, the judge, and the phenomenon protocol. It even named the tie-breaker, which was reproducibility â€” Arthur's own metric. Mercer had learned from the last exercise. He had built a contest on Arthur's terms, which meant he intended to win on neutral ground. That was the smartest thing he had done since the warehouse.

Arthur signed the acceptance and filed a copy with the board. He did not sign it with any confidence. He signed it because a challenge refused was a challenge remembered.

Before he signed, he read the protocol twice. The tie-breaker was reproducibility, which favored him. The judging was by a panel of three, two of whom were Combat instructors, which favored Mercer. The phenomenon was drawn by lot from a sealed list, which favored neither. The protocol was, on the whole, honest. It had been designed by someone who wanted the exercise to produce a true result, and Arthur suspected that someone was Mercer himself.

That was the part he could not dismiss. A man who designed a fair contest was not a bully. He was a person trying to prove something to himself, and he had chosen Arthur as the proof. Arthur wrote that in the parallel file and did not tell Clarke, because Clarke would have called it generosity, and it was not generosity. It was precision.

**Scene two â€” the lot.**

The phenomenon was an object-class Seep in a storage room. It returned tools to the last place a person had looked at them. Mercer's strategy was to mark the correct place first and let the Seep follow. Arthur's strategy was to stop looking.

"How do you solve it without looking?" Mercer asked.

"We look at the floor," Arthur said. "We let it return to the floor. Then we frame the floor."

Mercer laughed. "That is not solving. That is surrender."

"Surrender is faster."

They ran the exercise with two observers and a judge. Mercer went first. He marked a spot, stood on it, and looked hard at the tools. The Seep returned them to his feet inside four minutes. It was clean and fast and the observers nodded.

Arthur watched the observers more than the Seep. They wrote down Mercer's time, his marker position, and the moment of return. They did not write down the wear on the floor, because they had not been asked to, and the protocol did not require it. The protocol measured speed and containment, and Mercer had supplied both.

Arthur went second. He asked the observers to leave the room. He placed no marker. He stood at the door and looked at the ceiling, and then he closed his eyes for eleven minutes. When he opened them, the tools had arranged themselves around a drain cover in the floor.

"What were you doing with your eyes closed?" the judge asked.

"Removing myself as a witness." Arthur pointed at the drain. "The Seep follows the last place a person looked. If I look at the ceiling, the ceiling becomes a candidate. If I look at nothing, the last place is whatever place was last before I arrived. Which is the drain."

"That is a negative method."

"That is why it is reproducible. Anyone can close their eyes. Not anyone can find the drain."

The judge examined the drain cover. The wear around it was old and deep. He ruled Arthur's anchor location *correct* and Mercer's *coincident with the moment of observation*.

The Seep was contained by Arthur's frame at the drain. Both teams scored. The tie-breaker went to Arthur on reproducibility: his method could be repeated by anyone with the wear-reading, and Mercer's could not.

**Scene three â€” the draw.**

They ran the exercise a second time, two days later, with a different judge and a different storage room. This time Mercer watched Arthur's method and adapted. He did not mark a spot. He looked at the drain, the wear, and the floor, and he found the loss-anchor in six minutes.

"Your method is reproducible," Mercer said. "That was the tie-breaker. So I reproduced it."

"That is allowed."

"It is not a win. You gave me the method."

"I gave you the method the day you asked me to prove I could be wrong." Arthur watched the frame close. "You did not use it then. You used it now. That is progress."

Mercer did not answer. The judge ruled it a draw. The Seep was contained, but no one had located the anchor first.

Afterward, the board ruled as it had ruled before: Mercer first on the rank-weighted list, Arthur first on the reproducibility list. The two lists stood.

"A draw is not a loss," Mercer said.

"A draw is not a win," Arthur said. "You wanted data. Now you have less than before."

"Then I will run it a third time."

"The protocol allows two."

"Then next month." Mercer picked up his copy of the protocol. "I will request another. And another. Until the formula has enough data to decide."

"That is the formula's problem, not mine."

"It is your problem too." Mercer looked at him. "The formula will decide eventually. And when it does, it will not decide that we are equal. It will decide which one of us was the outlier."

"Then we both need the formula to be wrong."

Mercer was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "I have never heard you say 'we' before."

"Neither have I." Arthur gathered his papers. "It is accurate. The formula filed you as the benchmark and me as the anomaly. If either reading is wrong, both are wrong. That is the arithmetic."

Mercer looked at the empty storage room, then at the protocol in his hand. "Then I will keep running the test."

"I know."

"And you will keep winning it."

"I will keep solving it," Arthur said. "Whether that is winning depends on who is reading."

**Scene four â€” the data problem.**

Mercer watched him leave. "He does not win. He makes winning irrelevant."

Clarke, passing by, said, "That is a kind of winning."

"No," Mercer said. "It is a kind of defense. Defenses can be broken."

"Only if you attack the right thing."

Mercer looked at the empty storage room. "I will find the right thing."

Arthur heard the exchange from the corridor and did not turn around. He had learned, over six missions, that Mercer's frustration was not about him. It was about the formula that had made a D-rank into a position instead of a person. If Mercer broke Arthur, the formula would still be there. If Mercer joined Arthur, the formula would punish him. There was no third door, and both of them knew it.

But there was a fourth thing, and Arthur saw it clearly for the first time. Mercer had used Arthur's method in the second run, and the method had worked. That meant Mercer had learned something from an F-rank and had admitted it by using it. In the Academy's terms, that was contamination. In Mercer's terms, it was probably the most honest thing he had ever done, and he would never say so.

That night he wrote in the parallel file: **Mercer has learned the method. He has not learned the doubt. He will use the method better than the person who taught it to him, and he will still be filed as the benchmark, because the formula rewards the answer and not the question.**

Then he added a second line, which he did not intend to show anyone:

**He is closer to me than anyone in the Academy, and he would file that as an insult.**

He read the two lines and then added a third, because it was the part that mattered:

**The formula will decide one of us is the outlier. I know which one it will choose, and so does Mercer. That is why he keeps running the test. He is not trying to beat me. He is trying to change the formula's answer before it is written.**

That was the gamble. Mercer was not competing. Mercer was collecting evidence against a conclusion that had already been filed about him, and the only person in the Academy whose result could count as counter-evidence was the F-rank. He needed Arthur to win, repeatedly, publicly, in a way the board could not dismiss â€” because every one of Arthur's wins made Mercer's own rank less absolute.

Arthur closed the notebook. He had spent six weeks treating Mercer as a rival, and Mercer had spent six weeks treating Arthur as a witness. It was the same relationship viewed from opposite ends, and only one of them had understood it.
