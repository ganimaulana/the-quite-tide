# THE REMATCH EXERCISE

**Scene one â€” the misbrief.**

The second roster carried Pram's signature at the bottom of the request line. Arthur read it in the briefing room while the Combat students filed in behind him, boots louder than they needed to be. The room filled slowly, division by division. Combat took the front rows. Tactical took the side benches. Medical and Logistics stood near the door. Operations Support, as always, took whatever was left, which this time was a bench against the back wall with a broken leg.

"Exercise Redtail," the instructor said. "Simulated Seep in the north quarry. Object-class, low mobility. Contain and document."

Arthur looked at the page. The classification matched the briefing. The map did not. The briefing map showed the north quarry as a single depression with one access road. The file map â€” the one Arthur had copied before the briefing, the one filed in the annex three days ago â€” showed three drainage channels feeding into the quarry from the east ridge. If the Seep was object-class and low mobility, it should not have three possible approach routes.

He read the file map again. The channels were marked in a hand that had been corrected twice. Someone had added the channels after the original survey, which meant the briefing map was the older document, and the file was the newer one.

"Question," Arthur said.

The instructor looked at him. So did the Combat rows.

"The map has three channels," Arthur said. "The briefing says one approach."

"The briefing is correct," the instructor said.

"The file says otherwise."

"Then the file is from an older survey. Use the briefing."

"The file is newer," Arthur said. "The channels were added in a second hand. The briefing map predates the correction."

The instructor's pen stopped. For a moment, the room was very quiet. Then the instructor said, "The exercise is designed around the briefing. Use the briefing."

Pram was sitting three rows back. Arthur did not turn to look at him, but he could feel the attention, the same measuring attention from the courtyard.

"Yes, sir," Arthur said.

He sat down. Owen leaned over from the next seat. "It's wrong."

"Yes," Arthur said.

"What do we do?"

"We use the briefing. And we prepare for the file to be right."

Owen wrote something in his notebook. Clarke, on the other side, did not write anything. He only looked at the file map over Arthur's shoulder and whispered, "The channels are real. I can tell from the ink."

"Then we bring chalk," Arthur said.

**Scene two â€” the charter.**

The north quarry was colder than the Academy, and the wind carried dust down from the east ridge. Pram took point with two other Combat students, setting a perimeter around the object the briefing had placed at the quarry's center: a rusted pump housing the Academy staff claimed was the Seep anchor.

Arthur walked the perimeter in the opposite direction, Owen and Clarke with him. The truck that had brought them was parked at the access road, and the driver had already gone back. No one would be coming to help.

"Pram wants us to fail where everyone can see it," Owen said.

"He wants us to fail where he can see it," Arthur said. "That is not the same thing."

"It feels the same."

"It feels the same because he is good at choosing the place."

They reached the first drainage channel. Clarke knelt and touched the stone.

"Water moved here recently," he said. "The channel is active."

"The briefing said dry," Owen said.

"The briefing was wrong." Clarke stood and wiped his hand on his coat. "Water moves through this channel twice a day. The stone is worn smooth on the bottom. Whoever wrote the briefing never walked it."

Arthur looked back at the quarry. Pram's team was still at the pump housing, waving instruments and waiting for a tone that would not come. The Seep was not at the center. It was coming down the channels, drawn by the vibration of boots and voices, and it would reach the center from three directions at once.

"We need to spend their strength," Arthur said.

"Whose?" Owen asked.

"Pram's. The Combat students. Their strength is the perimeter. If we pull them to the channels, the Seep follows them. Then we document the actual anchor from the center."

"That's their plan," Clarke said. "They are already at the center."

"They are at the wrong center. The real anchor is in the channel bed. The pump housing is a decoy."

Owen looked at the ridge. "They will not move because we ask."

"They will move if the Seep moves toward them." Arthur opened his notebook. "Clarke, read the stone. Tell me which channel has the oldest water. Owen, tell me which direction the flicker is strongest. I will tell Pram where to stand."

"He will not listen," Owen said.

"He will listen if we are right."

"And if we are wrong?"

"Then we lose the exercise, and Pram gets his fair fight." Arthur closed the notebook. "That is the risk of being right in front of people."

---

**Scene three â€” spend someone else's strength.**

Pram met them at the second channel with his hand on his baton.

"The Seep is not at the pump," Arthur said.

"We know. Our instruments say the same thing."

"Your instruments say the pump is the anchor because the Academy staff put a tone generator in the pump. The real anchor is moving down the east channel. If you pull your perimeter back to the channel lip, the Seep will follow you."

"And then?"

"And then we document it from the pump. With the tone generator removed, the Seep will settle at the real anchor in the channel bed. You will be between it and the quarry."

Pram's jaw tightened. "You want us to be the bait?"

"I want you to be the boundary."

"Same shape."

"Different result."

Pram looked at the channel. He looked at his team, then at the pump housing, then at Arthur the way a person looks at a map they do not trust.

"If you are wrong, we fail."

"If I am wrong, you can say you told me so."

"That is not the same as being right."

"No," Arthur said. "It is only the same as being honest."

Pram almost smiled. "Fine. Boundary."

He signaled his team. They moved to the channel lip, boots heavy on the stone, and the Seep â€” whatever it was â€” turned toward them. Arthur watched the Combat students set their line. For a moment, he understood what Pram meant by a fair fight: the perimeter was real, the risk was real, and if the plan failed, the failure would land on the people standing in the wind.

Arthur did not watch them become the wall. He ran to the pump housing, pried the tone generator loose, and threw it to Clarke, who caught it and shut it down in one motion. The Seep hesitated. Then it continued down the channel, slower now, toward the place where the real anchor had been all along.

Owen wrote the flicker count. Arthur wrote the coordinates. The Medical student marked the site. The Logistics student built the frame.

The Seep was contained. The Combat students held the channel lip for eleven more minutes, until the frame was sealed and the tone was gone. No one was hurt. Pram did not call for relief.

**Scene four â€” the conversion.**

The after-action review was held in a classroom with windows that looked out at the quarry. The instructor read both reports: the Combat report, which described the perimeter action, and the Irregular report, which described the misbrief, the channel approach, and the tone generator.

"The exercise was graded on containment," the instructor said. "Both reports agree the Seep was contained."

"Because we spent the perimeter," Pram said.

"Because we used the perimeter," Arthur said.

The instructor looked from one to the other. "Explain the difference."

"Spending implies waste," Arthur said. "Using implies a plan."

"The plan was mine," Pram said. "The boundary was ours."

"The plan was the Seep's," Arthur said. "We used its movement."

Pram stood up. The room went quiet. He walked to Arthur and stopped in front of him, close enough that the Combat students at the back could have reached them in two steps.

"Next time," Pram said, "you tell me the plan before we reach the field."

"I tried," Arthur said.

"Next time, you tell me in a way I believe before I walk away."

He held out his hand.

Arthur looked at it. The hand was not friendly. It was not hostile. It was the hand of someone who had decided that a thing existed and was now going to account for it.

He shook it. Pram's grip was firm and brief, and he did not say anything else.

Pram walked back to the Combat side of the room. The instructor closed the folder. The Irregulars left together.

"That was grudging," Owen said.

"That was respect," Clarke said. "The grudging part will come later."

Arthur did not answer. He was already writing the next report, because the next report was the one that would matter. In the margin he wrote the word **boundary**, then crossed it out, then wrote **bait**. Both words were true. The exercise had taught him that a plan could be honest and still cost someone else, and that the cost was the part the report always left out.

He filed the report and went to the dining hall. Pram was not there. The Combat table was loud, and the Irregular table was quiet, and between them the room hummed with the ordinary sound of people deciding what to think.

Before they reached the dining hall, Pram caught up with them.

"Reed."

Arthur stopped.

Pram held out the folded briefing map.

"You were right about the channels."

"Yes."

Pram gave him a look.

"You could make that less irritating."

"I could."

"Do it next time."

Arthur took the map. "Next time, don't make me find out in the field that your team has not read the corrections."

One of the Combat students behind Pram laughed.

Pram did not.

"Fair," he said.

He pointed at the map.

"The east channel was not on our copy."

"Then your copy was wrong."

"Yes."

Arthur looked at him. The answer had come too quickly to be defensive.

"You are going to fix it?"

"I already asked for the corrected map."

"Good."

Pram folded his arms.

"There is something else."

"What?"

"You watched the channel instead of the team for most of the exercise. If someone had fallen, you would have noticed late."

The corridor seemed quieter.

Arthur looked down at the map in his hand.

He did not like the criticism because it was accurate.

"Then I need to watch both," he said.

Pram nodded once.

"That's the part I wanted you to hear."

He walked away.

Owen waited until he was out of earshot.

"He just taught you something."

"Yes."

"Does that mean he is your friend now?"

"No."

Clarke adjusted his folder.

"Good. Friends are harder to file."

Arthur looked at the corrected map.

The first version of the exercise had been about proving the file was right.

The second had been about using that fact without letting the fact become more important than the people around it.

That distinction stayed with him longer than the victory did.
