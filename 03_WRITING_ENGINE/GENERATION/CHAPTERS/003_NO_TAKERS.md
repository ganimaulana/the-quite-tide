# OPENING CEREMONY

The first-years entered Ravenscroft Academy together.

There was no earlier first-year group waiting for them. Every newly admitted student belonged to the same cohort, and every first-year began with the same starting point.

Arthur Reed entered with them.

The main hall was already full when he arrived. Rows of first-year cadets filled the lower seats. Faculty occupied the raised platform at the front. Behind them hung the Academy crest.

Arthur found an empty seat and sat down.

His badge carried three pieces of information.

ESPER RANK: F

CLASSIFICATION: IRREGULAR (PROVISIONAL)

A student nearby noticed the F and looked away.

The Headmaster stepped to the podium.

"Welcome to Ravenscroft Academy."

The hall settled.

"You have all arrived here as first-year cadets. Your admission assessment determined whether you could enter this Academy. From today onward, you will build a different record."

A screen behind him displayed three headings.

ADMISSION ASSESSMENT

ESPER CLASSIFICATION

ACADEMY PERFORMANCE RECORD

"Do not confuse them."

Arthur listened.

"The admission assessment was conducted in written, physical, and Esper components. Your Esper classification describes what the classification system measured. Your Academy Performance Record will measure what you demonstrate here."

Another screen appeared.

YEAR 1  -  FOUNDATION

YEAR 2  -  DEVELOPING SPECIALIZATION

YEAR 3  -  FIELD READINESS

YEAR 4  -  PROFESSIONAL TRANSITION

"You will not be expected to know everything today. You are expected to learn."

The speech moved through attendance, conduct, safety, restricted equipment, examination rules, field authorization, and the Academy's responsibility to students and the public.

The Headmaster then explained the Academy's training structure.

"You will study several disciplines before you are permitted to specialize. Combat, investigation, anomaly analysis, medicine, reconnaissance, equipment handling, and field procedure are all part of the foundation."

Arthur listened more carefully.

"Your Rank does not decide which lessons you can attend. Your Esper Power does not excuse you from learning the basics. Your Classification tells the Academy how your ability is currently understood. Your performance here will be built through training."

That made more sense to Arthur than assigning every student a permanent place on the first morning.

When the ceremony ended, the first-years gathered around the admission results display.

The first list showed admission assessment results.

Hale was near the top.

A student beside Arthur whispered, "Hale scored highest on the admission assessment."

Another student answered, "That is not Cohort Points."

A second notice appeared.

ACADEMY PERFORMANCE RECORD

ALL FIRST-YEAR CADETS: 0 COHORT POINTS

Arthur read it twice.

No one had points yet.

The Academy would begin adding them through classes, exercises, practical work, teamwork, and other student activities.

The next screen listed Esper classifications.

ARTHUR REED  -  F

IRREGULAR (PROVISIONAL)

A student behind him read it aloud.

"F?"

Arthur turned.

"Records," the student said.

"Yes."

The student seemed unsure whether that was good or bad.

Arthur was unsure too.

He had already learned that the answer depended on what question was being asked.

The campus map appeared next.

Records & Archives was listed under Academy Services.

Arthur noticed the distinction immediately.

It was a facility, not another label on his badge.

His orientation packet confirmed it.

No field authority.

No specialist clearance.

No assumption that an inexperienced cadet could perform a specialist's work.

Arthur folded the packet and put it away.

He had a Rank F.

Zero Cohort Points.

No reputation.

That was enough to start.
By the end of the first shared foundation session, the Academy had started recording another kind of result.

A board outside the training hall listed the **First-Year Cohort Performance Standing**. It was built from Academy Performance Record points: theory, physical assessment, practical exercises, teamwork, and other graded work. It was not an Esper ranking.

The first-year board was newly opened. Every name began from the same starting point.

A second notice sat beneath the list.

**Esper Rank is a separate annual classification. Cohort points do not directly raise or lower Rank.**

Arthur read it once and moved on.

The distinction was useful. The points measured what a cadet demonstrated at the Academy. Rank measured something else.

For now, Arthur had little of either record.---

The Records office opened onto a corridor that led toward the main training grounds.

Arthur stood at the Support office with four field packets and a slip from the morning coordinator. The coordinator had not explained the route. She had only pointed at the slip.

"Records office. East campus."

Arthur folded the paper and started walking.

Morning light fell across pale stone and the open training ground beyond it. A group of students crossed the square together in a tight group. Their Rank cords and Academy badges were easy to see from a distance.

He checked the signs before choosing a direction. The east buildings were marked clearly enough, and the movement of students gave him a second confirmation: most of the morning traffic was flowing toward the training grounds, while clerks and support staff were moving between the smaller buildings along the eastern side.

Arthur noticed the difference before he noticed the people.

Some students walked through the center of the courtyard as if it belonged to them. Others kept to the edges.

A student passing in the opposite direction glanced at Arthur's shoulder.

His eyes stopped at the gray Rank cord.

Then they moved to the Operations Support insignia.

The student looked away.

He had seen the same hesitation in smaller forms before. The Academy had simply made it easier to read.

He crossed the courtyard and followed the signs toward the east buildings.

Medical occupied a white building on the north side. Logistics was farther west, beside a loading bay where clerks moved crates beneath a covered roof. The training halls were larger and newer. Their doors were wide enough for equipment to pass through, and students came and went in groups.

Arthur passed a glass wall overlooking one of the training rooms.

It was a shared foundation session.

Students rotated through short physical and practical drills: sprinting between marked lines, controlled archery at fixed targets, supervised firearm practice behind a safety barrier, and paired combat drills with padded weapons.

The exercises were different, but the principle was the same. The Academy was measuring how a cadet moved, reacted, coordinated, and followed procedure under pressure.

At the far end of the hall, another lane tested controlled Resonance use. Cadets practiced drawing a small amount of power through the body before a sprint or weapon movement. Some gained a visible edge in acceleration or force. Others produced almost nothing. The instructor recorded the result separately from the physical time.

Arthur could feel something when he tried it during his intake assessment, but not enough to matter. His body was doing most of the work. The distinction was already becoming clear: physical ability was one measurement; Esper output was another.

Arthur watched the archery lane first.

An arrow left the bow.

His attention shifted to the running lane.

A cadet crossed the finish mark and turned sharply for the next repetition.

Then a training round cracked from the controlled range.

Arthur's attention caught the movement before the sound reached him.

He looked toward the combat lane.

A padded blade was already moving through the next exchange.

Movement was clear.

Meaning was not.

A group of Tactical students came around the corner at a run.

Their boots struck the stone in a steady rhythm.

Arthur stepped aside.

Motion Sense caught the sequence before he consciously counted it.

Left.

Right.

Left.

Six students.

The last one was half a step behind the others.

Arthur watched him recover the gap.

Even here, Motion Sense remained useful for one thing and almost useless for another. It could tell him that something was moving. It could not tell him why a person had chosen to move.

The routing office was at the end of a narrow corridor. Arthur recognized it from the building number on Margaret's slip before he saw the sign beside the door.

The clerk behind the window accepted the four files, checked Margaret's slip, and stamped each one.

"Grade-3 routing," Arthur read aloud, looking at the stamp before he picked up the files.

The clerk looked up from the last file. "You don't know what Grade-3 routing means?"

"No. I have not been given the routing rules yet."

"You will. The Academy expects you to learn them quickly."

The clerk pushed the files back toward him.

Arthur waited.

The clerk sighed.

The clerk tapped the stamp with one finger. "For now, it means one simple thing: do not lose any of these files, and do not send them to the wrong desk."

"I won't."

"Good. Then you understand the important part."

The clerk had already reached for the next file. The conversation was over because, as far as he was concerned, there was nothing else Arthur needed to know.

Arthur took the files and left.

On the way back, he stopped near the edge of the training ground. The exercise had drawn a crowd, and he wanted to understand why the route around the field was so busy.

The Combat students had changed exercises.

One of them threw a short training baton.

Another caught it cleanly.

The first student looked toward the path.

He saw Arthur.

"You're going the wrong way, clerk. The support block is behind you."

Arthur looked at the building behind him.

"I know the dining hall is past the training grounds. Which path takes me there without cutting through the exercise area?"

"Go past the support block and keep following the main path. You will see the dining hall on the left. The exercise area blocks the shorter route."

The student looked at Arthur's insignia again.

The student glanced at Arthur's badge. "That is where the Records students usually eat. Most of them take the tables at the edge."

Arthur looked from one student to the other.

"Thank you. That is much clearer."

The first student seemed almost disappointed.

"Sure."

Arthur continued walking.

He did not answer the insult. The student had given him useful information, even if he had chosen to attach contempt to it. Arthur preferred to keep the useful part and discard the rest.

By noon, he understood the Academy a little better. Nobody had given him an orientation, but he had already started building one for himself: the major buildings, the paths between them, and the flow of students.

The more useful information was social. People kept showing him where they believed he belonged.

The dining hall was a long room with a serving counter at one end and windows high enough to keep the afternoon sun from reaching most of the tables.

Combat students occupied the center.

They had bags on the spare chairs. Training jackets hung from the backs.

Research students clustered closer to the windows.

Support students filled the remaining spaces.

Arthur collected his tray and chose an empty chair near the edge of the room.

The boy across from the empty chair glanced at Arthur's badge. "You're assigned to Records?"

"Records. They assigned me to Academy Services."

The boy nodded toward the chair.

The boy gave a small shrug. "Around here, most people treat those as the same thing."

Arthur sat.

The girl across from him gave a short laugh. "Do not let him hear you say that. He spent yesterday explaining paper fiber as if the Academy had appointed him its guardian."

"I did not say it was sacred," the boy said. "I said the paper tells you whether a file has been handled properly."

The girl raised an eyebrow. "You spent ten minutes yesterday explaining the difference between two kinds of paper fiber."

"That was relevant to the condition of the file," the boy said. "The page had been replaced."

"It was relevant to you," the girl said. "Nobody else at the table was trying to inspect the paper."

Arthur ate a piece of fish.

The boy looked at him.

"You were in the assessment room last week?"

"Yes."

"The machine stopped while you were being tested, didn't it?"

Arthur looked up.

The boy shrugged.

"People noticed it. You were not the only person who saw the machine stop."

Arthur did not ask how many people had noticed.

He had already learned that information moved through the Academy faster than official notices.

The girl beside him glanced toward the entrance before lowering her voice.

"Were you the survivor from the Ravenhurst Massacre?"

Arthur stopped eating.

"Which survivor?"

"The one in the news last week. The boy who killed one of the monsters."

The boy across from him looked at Arthur again.

"That's you?"

Arthur put his fork down.

"Yes."

Neither student spoke for a moment.

The girl broke the silence first.

"I read that you were the only civilian found alive."

"That is what the report says."

"And you really killed one?"

Arthur looked at his own hand.

"I remember killing it."

The boy frowned. "But you're Rank F."

"Apparently."

The boy gave a short, uncertain laugh.

"Then how did you kill a monster?"

Arthur picked up his fork again.

"I don't know."

That answer spread through the table faster than the question had.

The girl beside him tapped her cup against the table.

The girl studied his shoulder cord. "You are Rank F, right?"

"Yes. That is what the assessment gave me."

She nodded.

She hesitated before adding, "I do not mean that as an insult."

Arthur waited.

She smiled.

She gave him an apologetic smile. "Actually, forget that. The way I said it made it sound exactly like an insult."

The boy laughed.

Arthur looked at her for a moment. "That was an efficient way to correct yourself."

She laughed harder.

The girl laughed. "You are a strange person, Reed."

"Probably," Arthur said. "I have been told that before."

The conversation moved on. It was not friendship, but it was not hostility either. For a few minutes, Arthur simply ate and listened to people talk around him.

Then a shadow fell across the table.

"So this is the table where the Irregular students have been sitting."

Arthur looked up.

Hayes stood there.

He was older than the students and wore the uniform differently. Nothing about his posture suggested that he needed to raise his voice to control a room.

"Sir," the girl said.

Hayes looked at the four students at the table.

Then at Arthur.

Hayes looked at Arthur. "You are the new Academy Services assignment."

"Yes, sir. That is my assignment."

Hayes's eyes dropped briefly to the gray cord on Arthur's shoulder.

Hayes's gaze moved from Arthur's badge to the training hall beyond the dining hall. "You are a records clerk in an Academy where most people expect problems to be solved by someone who can act on the field. That difference will matter if you forget what your assignment is for."

The boy with the fiber-memory ability smiled.

The boy with the paper-fiber ability smiled. "That sounded almost encouraging, sir."

Hayes ignored him.

Hayes looked back at Arthur. "Do the work you were assigned, Reed. You are good at noticing things, but do not make the mistake of thinking that noticing something means you understand it."

Arthur considered that.

"Understood, sir. I will keep the distinction in mind."

Hayes moved on.

The boy watched him leave.

The boy waited until Hayes was out of earshot. "That was the warm part of the welcome. He could have told you to leave the table. Instead, he only reminded you where you stand."

Arthur looked toward the center of the dining hall.

A Combat student had just pulled another chair into his group.

Nobody had asked whether it was available.

Nobody at the support tables looked up.

Arthur returned to his food.

The Academy had no official sign dividing the room. The social grouping was informal, but it was consistent.

After lunch, Arthur returned the empty tray and went back outside.

He took the long route toward the old gym.

There was no reason to hurry. The longer route gave him time to learn the campus.

The main academic buildings were taller than they had appeared from the entrance. Between them were smaller courtyards, covered walkways, storage buildings, and training areas that seemed to belong to different parts of the Academy.

Students moved constantly.

Some hurried toward classes.

Some carried equipment.

Some wore training gear.

Others looked as if they had spent the morning behind a desk.

Arthur passed a ranking board outside the new training hall.

Students were gathered around it.

He stopped.

Names were arranged by current **Cohort Points**. A smaller column beside each name carried the cadet's Esper Rank for reference, but the order on this board was determined by the Academy Performance Record.

Arthur found his own name below the first group. His Rank F had not placed him there. He simply had not earned many Academy points yet.

No one reacted. That was almost more revealing than an insult would have been. His Rank F cord was ordinary enough that people had already decided what it meant.

A student standing beside him noticed where he was looking.

"Do not stare at the ranking board for too long. People notice when someone keeps looking at the bottom."

"Why? What do people assume when someone keeps looking at the bottom?"

"You will start thinking the ranking tells you what you are worth here."

Arthur looked back at the board. "Then the ranking is not only measuring ability. People are using it to decide how they should treat one another."

The student shrugged. "To some people, yes. To others, it only tells them which cord you wear."

He walked away.

Arthur remained for another moment.

The board showed what the Academy measured. The dining hall had shown him how students translated those measurements into social distance.

He continued toward the old gym.

The building looked older than the rest of the east campus. Its wooden floor showed years of repairs, and several equipment racks stood half-empty along the wall.

A stores clerk sat beside a notice board.

Arthur handed over the dining hallage he had been carrying.

The clerk read it, stamped the receiving copy, and returned it.

Arthur turned to leave.

Then he noticed the board.

Most notices were ordinary.

Equipment checkouts.

Room changes.

A missing watch.

A request for a sparring partner.

A shower block closure.

One posting was different.

It was a field assignment.

The destination was in the hills.

The deadline was three days away.

The composition line had space for several students, but nobody had signed up.

Medical was listed as required.

The roster was empty.

Arthur read the notice twice.

A close deadline explained part of it. The distance explained more. The Medical requirement narrowed the pool further.

There was no need to invent a mystery yet. Those were already enough practical reasons for an empty roster.

The stores clerk noticed him.

The stores clerk looked up from the notice. "Are you interested in taking that assignment?"

"No. Not with the deadline this close."

"Then step aside. You are blocking the notice board for everyone else."

Arthur stepped aside.

He copied the assignment number into his notebook.

The clerk frowned.

The clerk watched him copy the number. "Why are you writing down the assignment number if you have no intention of taking it?"

"So I can find the notice again later. I want to compare the requirements with the roster if it stays empty."

"You just said you do not want the assignment."

"Not yet. I have not decided whether the assignment itself is the problem, or whether the timing is the problem."

The clerk shook his head. "Records people. You always keep the number even when you throw away the request."

Arthur put the notebook away.

He walked back toward the Academy Services archive.

On the way, he passed the training hall again.

The same foundation session was still running.

A runner crossed the marked lane.

An arrow struck a target.

A training round sounded from the controlled range.

Arthur turned toward the sound before he consciously registered it.

Arthur stopped.

He looked through the glass.

The instructor reset the lanes.

The next rotation began.

Movement first.

Sound second.

He watched three more repetitions.

Then he continued walking.

That evening, back in the Academy Services archive, Arthur found an unused notebook scheduled for disposal. The cover was blank and the paper was cheap, but that was enough for what he wanted.

He wrote one sentence on the first page:

**Motion Sense: information about movement, not meaning.**

He closed the notebook and put it in his locker. The sentence was a reminder of the boundary he had already tested: movement gave him information, but information did not automatically give him meaning.

Before leaving the Academy Services archive, he checked the copied assignment number in his regular notebook.

The number was still there. He looked at it once more, then closed the notebook.

Then he tested something more important.

He looked at the empty space above the desk and thought about the interface.

It appeared.

________________________________________
CONTRACT INTERFACE

USER
ARTHUR REED

NATURAL ABILITY
Motion Sense - ACTIVE

CONTRACT
None
________________________________________

Arthur waited.

Nothing else appeared.

He looked toward the other clerk.

"Can you see anything here?"

The clerk glanced at the wall.

"See what?"

Arthur shook his head.

"Nothing."

The clerk returned to the files.

Arthur closed the interface.

That was enough of a test.

The interface was private.

He would keep it that way.

The Academy had already shown him where it placed him.

The empty roster had shown him that some assignments were avoided for practical reasons. He did not know yet whether that posting was one of them.

---

The report arrived three days after the assessment.

Elias read it once.

Then again.

**ESPER RANK: F**

**NATURAL RESPONSE: MOTION SENSE**

**CLASSIFICATION: IRREGULAR (PROVISIONAL)**

He turned the page.

The rest of the assessment was more interesting than the result.

There had been a pause during the reading.

Three beats.

Long enough for the examiner to stop typing. Long enough for a second staff member to be called in. Long enough for the machine to produce a result that apparently explained nothing.

Elias closed the file.

He had seen Arthur beside the body.

He had seen the knife.

He had seen the wound.

What he had not seen was how a seventeen-year-old untrained civilian had created it.

His phone rang.

Elias looked at the screen before answering.

"Yes?"

"I heard you sent me a student."

Elias leaned back in his chair.

"I sent you a recommendation. Not a student."

"Same thing, once they arrive at my door."

The Headmaster's voice carried the faint amusement of someone who had used the same argument before.

Elias looked down at the report.

"Arthur Reed."

"The Ravenhurst survivor?"

"Yes."

"And you recommended him."

"I recommended an assessment."

"And now he's been assessed."

"Yes."

"Rank F."

Elias looked at the result again.

"That's what the paper says."

There was a short silence.

"You only send me paperwork when you don't trust the paperwork," the Headmaster said.

Elias almost smiled.

"I don't call you often."

"Exactly."

The Headmaster took a breath.

"Who is he?"

"A seventeen-year-old survivor. No training. Motion Sense. Irregular classification."

"That's the file."

"Yes."

"What did you see?"

Elias did not answer immediately.

"A dead monster."

"That's in the report."

"And a seventeen-year-old boy standing beside it."

"Also in the report."

"The report doesn't explain how."

Another silence.

Then the Headmaster said, "F rank?"

"Yes."

"Then perhaps F is exactly where he belongs."

Elias looked at the assessment sheet.

"You don't believe that."

"I didn't say I did."

Elias let out a quiet breath.

"I'm not asking you to solve it."

"Good."

"I'm asking you to give him somewhere to stand."

The answer came without hesitation.

"Ravenscroft can do that."

Elias looked through the office window at the city beyond the glass.

"Keep an eye on the file."

"I keep an eye on every file you send me."

"Not like this."

The Headmaster did not ask what he meant.

He already knew.

"Then we'll see what the boy does with the place."

The call ended.

Elias put the assessment back into its folder.

The official result was F.

He had no reason to dispute it.

He had a reason to remember what happened before it.
