The sealed files had been stored in the east wing of the records annex, where the retention period was measured in decades and the dust was older than most students. Arthur was sent to find a survey marker from a Drowning-year case. The request came through normal channels, but the file it named had been marked **SEALED â€” ACCESS BY COMMITTEE**.

The east wing was cold even in November. It had its own boiler, and the boiler had been off since the summer. Arthur worked with his coat on and his hands inside his sleeves, reading labels by the light of a single standing lamp that he moved from stack to stack.

He found the box anyway, because the sealed files were not lost. They were only hidden behind older boxes, which the annex staff had decided was the same thing. It was not the same thing. A hidden file could still be found by someone willing to move the boxes.

The case was from a winter before the Academy existed in its current form. A Seep in a ferry terminal had returned to the same ticket window every night for three months. The investigators had documented it, classified it, and then sealed the record. The reason for the seal was missing. The method was not.

Arthur read the method twice. It matched the thimble: isolate the object, observe the return, compare the intake record, wait. The words were different â€” the old file called it "anchoring by witness" â€” but the shape was the same. Someone in 1927 had solved the same class of problem with the same logic.

He copied it. Then he copied the seal page too, because the seal was part of the record, and a record without its seal would raise questions he could not answer.

Copying took an hour. The old carbon paper had dried out, and he had to press hard enough that his wrist ached. When he was finished he had six pages: the incident report, the method, the classification sheet, the closure note, the seal page, and a photograph of the ticket window with a date written on the back in pencil. The photograph showed a window that no longer existed. The date on the back was 1927.

He put the copies inside his coat and replaced the originals exactly, so that no one could tell the box had been opened. Then he wrote, in his own log, the only fact he would allow himself to carry out of the east wing: **One case, sealed, method compatible with Contract #1 behavior. Seal reason missing. Photograph exists.**

He did not write what he suspected, because a suspicion was not a record.

**Scene two â€” the knock.**

The terminal was still standing, converted into a storage depot for the riverside warehouses. Arthur went in person because the file said the Seep responded to presence, not to remote observation. He walked through the empty waiting room with the artifact in his pocket, the one he was not supposed to carry into the field, and waited.

The waiting room had been stripped: no benches, no clocks, no ticket window. But the ghosts of the room remained in the floor. There was a worn path from the doors to where the counter had been, and a second path from the counter to the platform stairs. The building remembered its own traffic.

The knock came after forty minutes. It was not a sound. It was a pressure in his chest, like a word spoken in a language he did not speak. His left hand went numb first, then his right. The artifact grew warm.

Arthur did not answer. The file said the previous investigator had answered on the third knock and the Seep had attached to his voice for a week. Arthur let it knock twice more, then he spoke the only thing he was sure of: "I read your record."

The knocking stopped.

The pressure in his chest eased. The artifact cooled by a degree, then another. The Seep had not expected a reader. In three months of Academy training, no one had told him what a Seep did when it was understood. They had told him what it did when it was attacked, contained, or ignored. They had not told him about the fourth thing, because the fourth thing was not in the manual.

He wrote in his notebook: **Response to reading: cessation. Duration: 40 seconds. Repeat: unknown.**

He waited another hour, standing in the middle of the room, to see whether the knocking would return. The building was quiet. The floor kept its worn paths. Twice he heard a sound from the street â€” a cart, a door â€” and both times the pressure in his chest began to build and then faded, as if the Seep had reached toward him and then decided against it. He wrote both events down, with times.

By the end of the second hour, his hands had gone cold from standing still, and he had learned three things. The Seep was still there. It recognized him. And it was waiting to see whether he would give it something to hold.

**Scene three â€” previous holder.**

In the margin of the old file, someone had written a note in a hand that had not been copied into the official summary. It named a method and a role, not a person: **previous holder â€” keep the intake record and wait for the return to match.**

Arthur read it three times. The note did not say who the previous holder was. It did not say what had happened to them. It only said that the method was older than the file, and that the file was older than the Academy.

He thought about the word *holder*. He had been called a clerk, an observer, an F-rank, a records body. No one at the Academy had used the word *holder*. The word belonged to the artifact, to the stone in his pocket, to whoever carried it before. It was the oldest word in the file and the only one that mentioned a person.

He took the note back and filed it beside the thimble record, the dormant docket, and the waterfront review. The previous holder was a thread now, not a name. The cold case was open only to the extent that a door could be open without a room behind it.

Hayes found him at the filing cabinet.

"You should not have gone alone," Hayes said.

"I had to be present. The file said so."

"The file says many things. The file does not say a student should go."

"I know."

Hayes looked at the copied note. "What is the previous holder?"

"A method."

"And the person?"

"Gone."

Hayes closed the cabinet. "That is the correct answer," he said. "For now."

"For now," Arthur repeated.

"The Academy keeps two kinds of files: the ones it wants people to read, and the ones it wants people to find. You have just found a file of the second kind." Hayes looked at the cabinet, then at Arthur. "The difference between the two is who is allowed to say they read it."

"Which kind is this one?"

"The kind that means someone is watching to see who opens it." Hayes turned to go. At the door he stopped, without turning around. "If you find the previous holder's name, do not write it where I can read it."

"Why?"

"Because I would have to file it."

**Scene four â€” the note in the file.**

That night, Arthur laid the copied pages out on the low table in the records block: the 1927 method, the seal page, the marginal note, and his own field record from the terminal. Four pages. One method. One role. One blank where a name should be.

He read them in order, then in reverse. The reverse reading told him something the forward reading had not. The marginal note was written in two hands. The first hand had written "previous holder." The second hand had added "keep the intake record and wait for the return to match." The second hand was newer, and it was the same hand that had corrected the map of the north quarry in the exercise file.

Someone at the Academy had read this file recently. Someone had added to it. And the addition described exactly what Arthur had done.

He did not know if the note had been written for him, or if he had simply done what the note described. It was the first time in his life that a piece of paper had frightened him. He folded it carefully and put it in the folder marked **PREVIOUS HOLDER â€” OPEN**, which he made that night and which had, at the time, only one page in it.
