"That is the same answer, in Institute terms." She smiled. "They will wait. I am not here on their behalf."

"Then whose behalf are you on?"

"My own. I have a question that is not in the offer."

Arthur waited.

"The Reed name in the 1934 file," she said. "I found it before you did. The Institute has been reading your filing habits."

"Why?"

"Because you file like a researcher, not a clerk. Most students copy what is in front of them. You cross-index. You keep parallel notes. You asked the archive for a map you were told not to use." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "The Institute noticed in October. I noticed in November."

Arthur did not deny any of it. Denying would have been a record too.

"What is your question?" he asked.

"What does the artifact do?"

He looked at her for a long moment. It was the first time anyone had asked the question plainly, without a form attached.

"It returns to recorded locations," he said.

"Which recorded location?"

"The last one that was accepted."

"And who accepts it?"\r\n\r\n"The authority responsible for the record. The keeper can submit it, but the record only counts when that authority accepts it."

She set down her cup. "Then the artifact is not a tool. It is a position."

Arthur felt the words land. He had been circling that sentence for four months. She had said it in one breath.

"What does a position require?" he asked.

"A keeper," she said. "A record. A line of succession, if the position is old enough to have one."

"And a cost?"

She looked at him over the rim of her cup. "Every position has a cost. The question is whether the cost falls on the keeper or on the people around them."

"Which is it?"

"I do not know. The records do not say. That is the part I want your help with." She leaned back. "The Institute wants to know what the artifact does so they can use it. I want to know what it costs so I can decide whether to stop them."

Arthur did not answer. It was the most honest sentence he had heard all term, and it had been spoken by a person who had just admitted she might not be able to keep her promise.

**Scene two â€” the archive below the river.**

The Institute's archive was a building with no name on the door. Dr. Kajiwara led him down a staircase that smelled of stone and old paper. The files were stored in rolling stacks, each one labeled by decade.

"So they know I am here," Arthur said.

"They know I am here. They do not know you are."

"Why show me this?"

"Because the Institute has three rooms like this one, and all three are catalogued, and none of them contains the file you need." She stopped at 1927. "This is the earliest one. A Seep at a ferry terminal. The same note. At first glance, the same hand."

Arthur read the record. The Seep had returned to a ticket window every night for a month. The investigation was closed as resolved. In the margin, the same clerk had written: **Reed.**

"What does it mean?" she asked.

"It means the same family name appears in three Seep records across sixty years. It does not mean it is the same person."

"No," she said. "But it does not mean it is not."

He read the 1927 record slowly. The ink was the same as the 1934 margin. The same clerk had signed both, which should have been impossible, because 1927 and 1934 were seven years apart and clerks did not usually keep the same posting that long.

"They look like the same hand," he said.

"Yes."

"Clerks rotate every two years."

"Usually." She looked at the stack. "Unless the clerk was not a clerk."

Arthur copied the records. He did not tell her about the artifact in his pocket. He did not tell her about the capacity marker. He only copied what she showed him.

**Scene three â€” the offer's shadow.**

Outside, the winter air was sharp. Dr. Kajiwara handed him a card with a private address.

"The Institute does not know I brought you here," she said.

"Why?"

"Because they want to own the answer. I want to understand it." She paused. "There is a difference."

Arthur put the card in his pocket, beside the artifact.

"There is also a difference between understanding and being used," he said.

"Yes. I am hoping you are good enough at filing to know which one I am."

She walked away. Arthur watched her go, then looked at the card. It had one line on it: an address and a time, and under them a single word, **THURSDAY**. No name, no number, no institution. A person who did not want to be found could not be found through that card, which meant the card was for meetings, not dining hallages.

He filed the card with the other open items. The break was almost over. The term would start again. And now he had three Seep records, one family name, one impossible clerk, and a researcher who was not sure if she was an ally.

The river went past the cafÃ© window in grey slabs. He watched it for a while, because the records under his hand were about a ferry terminal, and the ferry terminal had been on a river, and the river had been here longer than the Academy, longer than the Institute, longer than any name in any margin.

"One more thing," Dr. Kajiwara said, standing. "The Institute believes the previous holder is dead. They have believed it since 1974."

"Why 1974?"

"Because that is when the east storage block closed. The block kept the keeper's records. When the block closed, the Institute assumed the line ended." She put on her hat. "I have read the closure file. It does not say the keeper died. It says the keeper was *transferred*."

"Transferred where?"

"That page is missing." She smiled, not warmly. "Which is why I found you. You are good at missing pages."

**Scene four â€” the two hands.**

That night, on the train back to the Academy, he laid the copies out on the fold-down table and compared the handwriting with a magnifier he had borrowed from the archive.

The 1927 signature and the 1934 margin matched in every ordinary way: the slant, the spacing, the way the pen lifted after the family name. But there was one difference. The 1927 signature ended each stroke with a small rightward hook, and the 1934 margin ended each stroke with a small leftward hook.

Two hands. Same lettering. Same name.

Arthur put the magnifier down. Either the clerk had changed his hand over seven years â€” possible, since writing habits drifted â€” or two people had learned the same writing system and used it to sign the same name. The second explanation was strange. The first was ordinary. He filed both, side by side, under **HANDWRITING**, and noted that the ordinary explanation was the one he preferred and the strange one was the one he kept.

The train went through the dark. In his notebook, under the two entries, he wrote the sentence Dr. Kajiwara had given him, because it was the most useful thing anyone had said to him since September: *The artifact is not a tool. It is a position.*

If that were true, then the Reed name was not a coincidence. It was a title.

He closed the notebook and watched the last of the lights go past. The train was nearly empty. In the seat across the aisle, an old man slept with his coat as a pillow, and Arthur thought about the old worker from the warehouse, who had lost his fingers and had still come when asked. People carried their records in their bodies. The old man's missing fingers were a record. Arthur's memories of his dead family were records he could no longer verify with the people who had lived them. The keeper's missing page was a record of an absence.

The Institute wanted the artifact. Dr. Kajiwara wanted the cost. Arthur wanted the record, because a record was the only thing that could be read by someone other than the person who had lived it â€” and if the cost fell on the people around the keeper, then the record was the only way to warn them.

He wrote that down too, on the last page of the notebook, and then he put the notebook away and slept until the Academy.

**Scene five — the cost of a useful answer.**

Arthur left the café by the river and did not go directly back to the Academy.

He walked three streets north, stopped at a public records office, and requested the oldest available ferry district map.

The clerk behind the counter looked at the request. “Research?”

“Yes.”

“Academic?”

Arthur considered the question. “Not yet.”

The clerk stamped the form anyway.

The map showed the district before the current warehouse existed. The pier had moved twice. The storage buildings had been rebuilt. One narrow service road had disappeared entirely.

Arthur copied the changes rather than the map itself.

That was the useful part.

When he reached the Academy, he placed Kajiwara’s statements beside his own notes.

She had given him information. She had also told him what the Institute believed.

Those were not the same thing.

If Kajiwara was right, the Institute had been watching the ferry records for years. If she was wrong, she had still revealed what she wanted him to believe.

Arthur wrote one sentence beneath the two possibilities:

*A useful answer is still evidence about the person giving it.*

He closed the notebook.

The next time he met Kajiwara, he would bring a question she could not have prepared for.

That would tell him more than another answer.
