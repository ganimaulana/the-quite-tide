# CONTRACT TWO SURFACES

**Scene one â€” the new term.**

The Academy reopened on a grey morning. The first announcement was a new training schedule. The second was a notice about re-sorted students. The third, which appeared on the board outside the dining hall, was a mission request from the Institute.

Arthur read it three times.

It did not name him. It named a phenomenon: an object-class Seep in the old ferry district, the same district as the cold case. The request asked for a Joint Team with records support. The Institute would provide a liaison.

The liaison's name was Dr. Kajiwara.

He read the third notice again, more slowly. The Institute had routed the request through the Academy, which meant the Academy had approved it, which meant someone in the Academy's chain of command had decided that the ferry district was worth a Joint Team. That was a decision the Academy did not make lightly in January, when the budget was still being argued.

"Same district as the cold case," Clarke said, reading over his shoulder.

"Same district. Different phenomenon."

"You think it is a coincidence?"

"No," Arthur said. "I think it is a chain."

Owen came up beside them with his notebook already open. "I checked the term schedule. The terms have no ferry missions. Not for ten years."

"Why not?"

"Because the ferry district was closed as a training area in 2014. Too many Seeps, too few results." Owen tapped the notice. "This mission is the first one back."

Arthur filed the three facts together: same district, ten-year gap, first mission back. A mission that came back after ten years came back because someone had decided the risk was acceptable. He wanted to know who had decided.

**Scene two â€” the adversarial phenomenon.**

The Seep was not in the water. It was in a warehouse that had been built over a filled-in pier. It returned objects to the last place they had been seen, which meant every tool, glove, and shoe left in the building migrated back to the front door within hours.

The building was long and cold. Its floor was stained with two decades of rust and oil, and the front door had a brass plate worn smooth by hands that had opened it when the warehouse was new.

"Same family as the thimble," Owen said. He had been assigned because of his flicker-log. "First-intake behavior."

"Not quite," Arthur said. "The thimble returns to the first accepted record. This one returns to the last place something was seen."

"What's the difference?"

"Memory. This Seep follows witnesses, not records."

Clarke crouched by the door. "Then we cannot solve it by filing. We need to change what it remembers."

"Or who remembers it," Arthur said.

He walked the length of the warehouse and stopped at a spot in the middle of the floor where the stain was darkest. The floor there was worn in a pattern: a patch of blank concrete surrounded by scuff marks that all pointed inward.

"Tools were laid here," he said. "Often. And taken from here. The Seep returns them to the door because the door is where the workers last saw them."

"How do you know?" Pram asked.

"Because the scuff marks point inward at the patch but outward at the door. Inward means things were set down. Outward means things were carried away." Arthur straightened. "The door was the last witness. The patch was the first record. We need to make the Seep choose the patch."

"Why would it?"

"Because we will become the stronger witness. A hundred workers passing through a door left a weak memory. Six of us standing on the patch, doing nothing, leaves a stronger one."

"That is a guess," Pram said.

"It is a plan. The guess is which spot."

Pram looked at the patch, then at the marks, then at Arthur. He did not argue. He walked to the patch and stood on it, arms folded, facing the door. He did not ask what to do next. He simply made himself a witness, and waited.

Owen joined him. Then Clarke. Then the Medical student, who had been standing near the door with her kit, and the Logistics student, who had been measuring the frame line. One by one, the team moved onto the patch and became, together, the strongest memory in the room.

Nothing happened for eleven minutes. Then the tools in the corner â€” a wrench, a pair of gloves, a broken-handled hammer â€” slid across the floor, not toward the door, but toward the patch. They stopped at Pram's boots.

"Witness accepted," Arthur said.

"For how long?" Pram asked.

"Until we move."

Pram did not move.

Dr. Kajiwara stood in the corner, taking notes. She did not interfere. She only watched, and when Arthur looked at her, she looked back without any particular expression, which told him she was recording both the phenomenon and the method.

Pram stepped off the patch and moved to the far end of the warehouse, taking the door as his watch position. He had stopped arguing with Arthur after the fourth mission, but he had not stopped watching.\n\n"Pram," Arthur said. "I need the door watched. If anything moves toward it, say so."

Pram nodded once. It was the shortest agreement of their acquaintance.

**Scene three â€” the artifact notices.**

Arthur felt the artifact warm against his leg. Not a hollow filling, not the pull of a spent seat â€” just warmth, the way the stone had warmed the first week, when it was only a stone. The first Contract had cost him enough: the mark, the near-drain, the flicker-log at the Quiet Desk. He was not looking for a second.

But the Seep was looking at him â€” or at the artifact â€” and the artifact seemed to answer backward.

He put his hand in his pocket.

The Seep stopped moving.

No one else saw it. No one else felt it. But the Seep paused mid-return, as if it had recognized something, and then it went back to the front door.

Arthur took his hand away.

The artifact had noticed the Seep. That was all he could say with certainty â€” a recognition, not a binding. Nothing had been offered, nothing had been sealed. He had learned enough in the annex to know that the stone could be interested in a thing without taking it, the way an appraiser could look at a carving and decline to value it. He took out his notebook and wrote the time, the location, and the exact sequence, using the plainest words he had.

"What was that?" Pram asked from the wall.

"Nothing yet."

"That was not nothing. The room changed for a second."

Pram had seen it. Arthur looked at him for a long moment. Four months ago, Pram would have said nothing and filed a report about it. Now he had said it out loud, to Arthur, before writing it down. That was a change in the record.

"The artifact recognized the Seep," Arthur said. "It has not bound anything. It only noticed."

"That sounds like the beginning of a problem."

"It is the beginning of a question."

Dr. Kajiwara wrote faster. Arthur said nothing more. He had already decided not to test the artifact's interest in front of the Institute, because a recognition performed in front of them would become a document, and a document would become a decision made by someone who was not him.

**Scene four â€” the terms.**

That night, the team debriefed in a classroom with the heating off. The Academy had not yet turned the heat back on for the spring term, and everyone worked in their coats.

"The Seep is not a thimble," Arthur said. "The thimble followed records. This one follows witnesses. If we write the right record, the Seep ignores it. If we stand in the right place, the Seep follows us."

"Then we stand in the wrong place on purpose," Pram said.

"Yes."

"And we write a record the Seep cannot see."

"Yes."

Pram looked at him. "You are describing a trap for something that watches people."

"I am describing a trap for something that watches people."

"That is not the same as capturing it."

"No. It is the same as understanding it first." Arthur closed his notebook. "The last phenomenon took us three weeks. This one is already in the Institute's request, which means the Institute expects it to be harder."

"Does it?"

"It expects it to be documentable." Arthur looked at Dr. Kajiwara at the back of the room. "That is what they came for. Not the Seep. The method."

She did not deny it. She only wrote.

Outside, the ferry district was dark. The river ran past the end of the street, and beyond the river the city put up its lights. Arthur stood at the window and thought about the difference between a phenomenon and a request. A phenomenon was a thing that happened. A request was a thing that someone had decided to write down. The Institute had written this one down before the team had even seen the Seep, which meant the Institute already knew something about the ferry district that the Academy did not.

He made a note to request the Institute's pre-mission file. He did not expect to get it. He expected the request to produce a refusal, and the refusal to produce a date, and the date to produce a name.

That was how the chain worked. He had learned it from the dormant docket, and the dormant docket had not yet failed him.

**Scene five — the second observation.**

Arthur returned to the warehouse alone after the team had gone.

He did not take the artifact out.

He stood at the front door and watched dust settle in the grooves of the floor.

The Seep had reacted to the artifact once. That did not mean the artifact controlled it. It did not even mean the two phenomena belonged to the same system.

Arthur wrote those sentences down because the temptation to connect them was stronger than the evidence.

Then he placed a glove on the floor and walked away.

Nothing happened.

He returned ten minutes later. The glove remained where he had left it.

He moved it to the patch where the team had stood. It remained still.

Then he moved it to the front door.

After eleven minutes, it slid three inches toward the threshold.

Arthur smiled despite himself.

The Seep was consistent.

That was more valuable than being spectacular.

He wrote the result into the field log and added one final line: *Do not test the artifact again until the ordinary rule is established.*

He left the warehouse with the question still open.

For once, that felt like progress.
