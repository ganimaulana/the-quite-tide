**Scene one â€” the report.**

Dr. Kajiwara came to the Academy the next morning with two copies of the mission report. One was for the Academy. One was for the Institute.

"They are watching you now," she said.

"Who?" Arthur asked.

"The Institute's acquisition board. They read the mission report. They know you solved a Seep Mercer could not."

"Mercer's team contained it after I showed them the first memory."

"Yes. But you showed them. That is what the report says." She paused. "They are going to offer you more than a research attachment."

"What?"

"Money. Rank. Protection. Whatever you need to stop being a student and start being their asset."

Arthur looked at the river. "I do not want rank."

"They know. They will offer it anyway. They will offer it because they think they understand what you want."

"What do I want?"

"To keep filing. To keep understanding. They will offer you an archive larger than the Academy's." She handed him the report. "Do not accept the first number."

He read the Institute's copy. It was almost identical to the Academy's, except for one section at the end: *Assessment of subject's methodology â€” provisional.* The provisional assessment was three paragraphs long, and it described his method accurately, and it ended with a recommendation to the board: *Acquire access to the subject's working files.*

They did not want him. They wanted the parallel file he had never shown anyone.

He read the provisional assessment once more, then folded it and put it in the parallel file. The Institute had made the same mistake the Academy made: they had assumed that the value was in the method, when the method was only the visible part of the thing. The value was in the cross-index. The method could be copied by anyone who read the appendix. The cross-index could only be built by the person who had lived the file.

That was the difference between a clerk and a keeper. A clerk stored what he was given. A keeper decided what could be found. He wrote the sentence in the parallel file and did not show it to Dr. Kajiwara, because Dr. Kajiwara answered to the board.

**Scene two â€” the sealed record.**

The Institute sent a sealed record along with Dr. Kajiwara. It was a file from 1927, the year of the first Reed note. It described a Seep at a different ferry terminal. The Seep returned passengers to the gate where they had first bought a ticket.

Arthur read it standing, because there was no chair in the corridor.

The file had a handwritten addendum: **Artifact present at site. Bearer Reed. Refused to sell. Refused to explain.**

He read it three times.

Artifact. Bearer. Refused to sell.

The artifact in his pocket was warm.

"There it is," Dr. Kajiwara said. "The first time the Institute tried to buy it."

"1927."

"And every generation since. They have tried eleven times. Each time, the bearer refused."

"What happened to the bearers?"

She was quiet for a moment. "The Institute's records say the line ended in 1974. But the 1975 transfer slip you found says otherwise."

Arthur looked at the addendum again. Eleven attempts. Eleven refusals. And a line that had gone north in 1975 and stopped writing.

"Why show me this now?" he asked.

"Because the board has stopped asking for the artifact," she said. "Now they are asking for the *bearer*. That is a different request, and I do not like it."

"Why?"

"Because an artifact can be stored. A bearer can be replaced." She looked at him. "The Institute has spent a hundred years trying to buy the stone. They have finally realized they do not need to. They only need to identify the person who holds it, and wait."

"Wait for what?"

"For the holder to fail, or refuse, or die." She said it flatly. "Then the stone passes to the next bearer, and the Institute offers again. That is how they have operated for eleven generations. They are patient because they do not have to do anything but file."

Arthur looked at the addendum in his hand. Eleven attempts. Eleven refusals. A hundred years of filing.

"Then I am not being recruited," he said. "I am being monitored."

"Worse. You are being *catalogued*."

**Scene three â€” the previous holder's identity.**

He copied the addendum and put it with the other Reed notes. The previous holder was not one person. It was a chain of people who had carried the same artifact. Each one had refused to sell it. Each one had been named Reed, or had been associated with someone who was.

Arthur wrote a new note: **Previous holder is not a person. It is a line of succession. The artifact passes. The name stays.**

Dr. Kajiwara read it over his shoulder.

"You are not surprised," she said.

"I am not surprised."

"Does it scare you?"

"No. It means there is a record."

She smiled. "That is the answer of someone who has not yet found the part of the record he does not want to see."

He looked at her. "What part?"

She did not answer. But she took the 1927 file and turned to the last page, the one he had not copied, and pointed at a name in the witness column. It had been written in the same two-handed style: a signature with a rightward hook, and beside it, a correction with a leftward hook.

The name was underlined. It was Reed. And beneath it, in the same leftward hand, someone had written a single date: **20 September**.

His birthday.

**Scene four â€” the offer's shape.**

Arthur went back to the records block and sat at the low table for a long time without writing anything.

20 September. He had been born on 20 September. The date had been written in a file before he was born, in the margin of a note about a bearer who had refused to sell an artifact in 1927.

It might be a coincidence. There were 365 dates. But the date had been written by the same hand that had corrected the clerk's signature, which meant it had been written by someone who knew both the clerk and the future. That was not a coincidence. That was a filing.

He did not tell Clarke. He did not tell Owen. He wrote the date in the parallel file, on a page by itself, and then he closed the parallel file and locked it in the bottom drawer with the rest.

But he could not stop thinking about the two hands. If one hand had written the clerk's name in 1927 and the other had corrected it in 1934, and if a third had written 20 September in the margin, then the file had been added to for at least eighty years. And every addition had been made by someone who knew more than the last.

That was not a file. That was a conversation. Someone had been writing to the future, in the margins of sealed records, for eighty years, and Arthur had finally started reading it.

The question was whether the conversation was a warning or an instruction.

The Institute's offer was on his desk, still open, still waiting. He looked at it for a while. Then he took out a fresh sheet and began a letter. He did not accept. He did not decline. He asked the board one question, in the plainest language he could manage: *Which bearer did the Institute last attempt to purchase, and when?*

He sealed the letter and sent it. The reply would take three weeks. He would spend those three weeks doing what he always did: filing, observing, and waiting for the person who had written 20 September to make a mistake.

Before he left the records block, he did one more thing. He took the parallel file out of the bottom drawer and counted the pages. Nine documents, four names, three dates, and one birthday. He had started the parallel file in November, and in two months it had become the most complete record of the artifact's history that existed anywhere â€” including the Institute's archive.

That was the danger. A record that complete was worth taking, and the Institute had already asked for it twice. He put the file back and locked the drawer, and then he did something he had not done before: he wrote a decoy summary, accurate but incomplete, and left it in his ordinary file where the next person to search his desk would find it.

A decoy was not a lie. It was a filing decision. It decided what could be found.

That night, he lay awake and did the arithmetic one more time. Eleven attempts to buy the artifact. Eleven refusals. One line that went north in 1975. One date written in a margin before he was born. And a clerk who appeared in 1927, 1934, 1975, and 1978 wearing two hands and one name.

Somewhere in that arithmetic was a person, alive or dead, who had been keeping the file current. He could not see them. He could only see their handwriting, and the handwriting had been waiting for him.

He slept, and in his sleep he did the thing he did when he was tired: he cross-indexed. He filed the date under three headings â€” FAMILY, ARTIFACT, WARNING â€” and he woke up with the three headings still in his head.
