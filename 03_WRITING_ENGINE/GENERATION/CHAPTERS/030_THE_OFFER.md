# THE OFFER

**Scene one â€” the institute.**

The letter arrived on Academy letterhead, but the return address was an institute across the city. It offered Arthur a research attachment for the term break. The language was polite, institutional, and careful. It did not say what he would be studied for.

Arthur read it in the records block, at the low table, with the December light coming through the high windows. The letter was two pages long. The first page described the institute. The second page described the terms. There was no third page, which was itself a kind of dining hallage: everything they wanted him to know was on two pages, and everything they did not want him to think about had been left out.

He read it three times. The first time, he looked for the catch. The second time, he looked for the price. The third time, he looked for the question they were asking.

The catch was in the third paragraph. The attachment would remove him from the Irregular roster for the duration. The price was in the fourth paragraph. The Institute would have first access to his records. The question was never stated.

He noticed the letterhead before he noticed any of the words. It was an Academy letterhead, but the seal at the top was the Institute's. Someone had printed the letter on Academy paper to make it look like an internal matter, and the seal had given it away. That was the first piece of information in the letter, and it was the piece they had meant to hide.

He put the letter in his pocket and went back to work.

He worked until the light changed. The low pile was bigger than it had been in September, because the term's exercises had generated more paper, and the paper all came to him. He copied, cross-indexed, and filed. Twice he stopped to read the letter again, and twice he put it away without deciding anything. Deciding was not the task. Deciding was what the letter was designed to make him do quickly, and anything designed to be done quickly at the Academy was usually designed to be done wrong.

**Scene two â€” Hayes's caution.**

Hayes found him on the roof of the east wing, where students were not supposed to be. He did not tell Arthur to come down.

"You have an offer," Hayes said.

"Yes."

"It is not a promotion."

"I know."

"They will study you. They will call it research. They will file you as a promising anomaly and decide what you are useful for."

"I know."

Hayes stood beside him. The city was grey below, and the river was a dull line between the buildings.

"I have been your advisor because the Academy needed someone to sign forms. I am telling you now, as a person, not to accept the first offer."

"It is the only offer."

"It is the only offer so far." Hayes looked at the city. "The Institute is not evil. It is efficient. It will use you cleanly, which is worse than using you cruelly."

"How is it worse?"

"Cruel use leaves marks. You can point at a mark and say, that happened to me. Clean use leaves nothing. You wake up one day and realize you have been useful for years and no one has ever had to apologize for anything." Hayes's voice was flat. "That is how the Academy treats its clerks. The Institute will treat you the same way, except they will pay you."

"What should I do?"

"Delay. Re-sorting is a decision that does not need to be made today. The Academy will let you defer. The Institute will wait, because waiting costs them nothing."

Arthur took the letter out of his pocket. "And if they do not wait?"

"Then you will know what the offer was really worth."

Hayes was quiet for a while. Below them, a line of students crossed the quadrangle in the cold, going from the dining hall to the residential block. None of them looked up.

"There is a second reason to delay," Hayes said.

"What?"

"The offer is not about you. It is about the artifact. They want the artifact, and they have decided that buying the holder is cheaper than seizing the object. If you accept, you become the receipt." He looked at Arthur. "A receipt can be filed and forgotten. That is a safe life. Some people would take it."

"I know."

"But you want to know what the artifact is, and a receipt is not allowed to ask."

**Scene three â€” the terms.**

Arthur took the letter to the records office and asked for the standard deferral form. The clerk behind the window looked at the letter, then at him, then at the letter again.

"You are deferring the Institute?"

"Yes."

"You are the first person to defer them in eleven years."

"Then the form exists."

The clerk found the form. It was a single page, and it had not been revised since before either of them was born. Arthur filled it out in the records block and attached a request for clarification: he wanted the Institute to state, in writing, what the attachment would require of him week by week.

He wrote the request carefully. He did not ask what they wanted to study. He asked what they wanted him to do. It was the same question in the opposite direction, and he wanted to see which direction they would answer.

While he filled out the form, the clerk watched him with the particular attention of someone who had been told to report anything unusual about this student. Arthur filed the observation under **WATCHED**, third entry. The first entry had been the auditor. The second had been the junior board member. Now the records clerk. The watchers were multiplying, and none of them had been rude about it, which made them harder to name.

He handed the form through the window. The clerk stamped it, filed it, and said, "The deferral form does not guarantee the Institute will accept a deferral."

"I know."

"Then why use it?"

"To make the refusal documentable," Arthur said. "If they accept, the form records the acceptance. If they refuse, the form records the refusal. Either way, the offer stops being something people can talk about and becomes something people can read."

The clerk looked at him for a moment, then slid the carbon copy across the counter. "Keep this one," he said. "Most students don't."

Arthur kept it. He had learned in the annex that the carbon copy was the version a person could prove existed.

**Scene four â€” the deferred sort.**

Arthur wrote a letter to the Institute. He thanked them. He asked for clarification about the terms. He did not accept. He did not decline.

He filed the original offer with the request for clarification. The Academy's records office stamped it DEFERRED and told him to expect a response before the break.

That evening, he sat in the dining hall with the Irregulars. Clarke pushed his folder across the table.

"You are not leaving," Clarke said. It was not a question.

"Not today."

"Today is the only day that matters."

"No," Arthur said. "Tomorrow is also a day."

Clarke almost smiled. "Then we will file today as deferred."

Owen looked up from his notebook. "If they answer with a schedule, you will know they want your time. If they answer with a description, you will know they want your behavior. If they answer with nothing, you will know they want you."

Arthur looked at him. "That is a good test."

"I write down patterns," Owen said. "It was only a matter of time before I wrote down a useful one."

Arthur ate his dinner and listened to the others talk. The offer was open. The re-sorting was open. The only thing that was closed was today, and today he was still here.

At the end of the meal, he made a note in his notebook. The Institute's reply would arrive in three days at the earliest and three weeks at the latest. He wrote both dates in the margin, and left the space between them blank, because the blank was where the answer would go.

That night, before he slept, he did something he had not done since the first week. He took the artifact out of its pouch and set it on the desk. It was warm, as always, and on its surface he could see the marks of the binding points: one filled, eleven empty, and the thirteenth side with its unreadable sign.

He counted the empty points. Eleven. He had started the term with twelve untouched markers and an F-rank file. Now he had one filled point, a mark on his hand, two reports in dispute, a method in the appendix, three Reed records, and an offer he had deferred.

The term had changed the numbers. It had not changed the rank.

He put the artifact back in its pouch and went to sleep. In the morning, the request would be in the Institute's inbox, and the deferral would be in his file, and the blank between the two dates would be waiting for whatever the Institute decided to write.

---

**Scene five — the answer to the question.**

The Institute replied three days later.

Arthur opened the envelope in the records block.

The letter was shorter than the first one.

That told him more than the contents did.

They had answered his request for a weekly schedule. They wanted two afternoons per week, access to his operational reports, supervised observation during approved Seep missions, and a term-end assessment. The assessment would be shared with the Academy.

They had not answered why they wanted him.

Arthur read the last paragraph twice.

The Institute would accept his deferral for one term.

Only one.

He took the letter to Hayes.

"They agreed," Arthur said.

Hayes read it. "To the deferral."

"Yes."

"And now they have given you exactly what you asked for."

Arthur nodded. "Which means the useful part is not what they gave me."

"What is it?"

Arthur pointed at the shared assessment. "They want the Academy to see their result."

Hayes leaned back. "That is normal for an attachment."

"Then why did they not say it in the first letter?"

Hayes did not answer immediately.

"Because the first letter was an offer," Hayes said at last. "This one is a negotiation."

Arthur looked at the two letters side by side.

The first had tried to make acceptance feel immediate.

The second had accepted his delay and supplied terms.

That meant the delay had worked.

It had not made the Institute lose interest. It had forced them to become specific.

Arthur folded the second letter.

"I will accept the attachment next term, subject to one condition."

Hayes looked up. "Which is?"

"I choose which records they receive. They can observe approved missions. They cannot take unrestricted access to my personal files."

Hayes was quiet.

Then he nodded. "Put it in writing."

Arthur smiled faintly. "That was the plan."

He returned to the records office and began drafting the amendment.

The Institute had wanted an answer.

Arthur had given them a contract to negotiate instead.

For the first time, the offer no longer felt like something being done to him.

It was becoming something he could shape.
