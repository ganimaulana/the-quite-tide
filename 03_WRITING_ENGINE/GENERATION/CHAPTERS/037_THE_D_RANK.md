# RIVAL INTRODUCTION

**Scene one  the D-rank.**

The rival was named Mercer. He was tall, D-rank, and had been assigned to the warehouse mission as the benchmark student. The Academy liked to include a benchmark in exercises involving irregular results. It gave them a number to compare against.

Mercer arrived at the debrief with his file already read. He had a copy of Arthur's method summary, which meant he had asked for it through channels, which meant he had known about Arthur before the assignment.

"You are the F-rank who files things."

"Yes."

"I read the report. Your stone answered a Seep in the ferry district."

"I did not decide to form it."

"That is worse." Mercer smiled. "A power you cannot control is not a power. It is a condition."

Arthur did not answer. Mercer was not wrong. He was also not right in a way that mattered. A Contract he had not chosen was still a Contract, and the fact that it had chosen him was information, not weakness. What had happened with the Seep was not even that much  a recognition, no terms, no new Contract formed. Mercer had read the report and made it larger than it was. But Mercer had not asked for information. He had asked for a reaction, and Arthur did not give him one.

"The exercise is on Friday," Mercer said. "I am the benchmark. You are the anomaly. The board wants to see which reading holds."

"Which reading do you think holds?"

"The one with the rank on it." Mercer tapped his own pin. "I have spent three years earning this. You have spent four months filing. The Academy does not give D-ranks to people who cannot control their own power."

"I do not control the artifact," Arthur said.

"Then what do you do?"

"I read it."

Mercer's smile thinned. It was not the answer he had wanted, and it was not an answer he could dismiss.

---

**Scene two  the public spectacle.**

The demonstration day was held in the main hall. Each team was given a phenomenon to solve in front of the assembled students. Mercer's team went first. They solved their phenomenon in four minutes. The crowd applauded.

Arthur watched from the side. Mercer's method was good: a standard containment, cleanly executed, with a D-rank's command presence. He had the room from the first sentence. That was a kind of power Arthur did not have and did not want.

But Arthur also watched the crowd, because the crowd was the other phenomenon in the hall. When Mercer spoke, the students leaned forward. When the containment sealed, they applauded before the judge had ruled. The applause was not for the method. It was for the certainty of the man delivering it. Mercer believed he would succeed, and the room had caught it.

That was the difference between them, and Arthur wrote it down while the next team set up: **Mercer's power is belief. Mine is doubt. The room prefers belief.**

It was not a self-pitying observation. It was a practical one. A room that preferred belief would need a different kind of proof than a technical demonstration. It would need a result that belief could not explain, shown twice, so that the second showing cost something. He filed the thought and watched the crate.

Arthur's team went last. The phenomenon was the warehouse Seep, now transported to the Academy in a warded crate. It was supposed to return objects to a marked point. It returned them to the crate's door.

"It remembers the warehouse door," Arthur said.

"Then make it forget," Mercer called from the crowd.

Arthur ignored him. He placed a chair in the center of the hall. He asked Owen and Clarke to look at it. Then he asked the old worker to look at it. Then he held up the artifact.

The Seep paused.

"It remembers the artifact," Arthur said. "Not the warehouse. Not the door. The artifact is the older witness."

The objects in the crate slid toward the chair.

The crowd was silent. Then someone clapped, uncertainly. The applause did not spread. Mercer did not clap. He was watching the crate with the expression of a man recalculating a number.

"Again," Mercer said. "Do it again."

"Why?"

"Because I want to see whether it works twice."

Arthur did it again. The objects slid toward the chair again. The second time, the crowd's silence had a different quality. It was the silence of people who had seen a rank stop mattering for four minutes and did not know where to put the information.

Mercer asked for a third demonstration, and the instructor refused.

"Two is the limit," the instructor said. "Any more and we are not testing the phenomenon. We are testing the crowd."

Mercer accepted that, but he kept his eyes on the crate for the rest of the session. Arthur understood what he was doing. He was not watching the Seep. He was watching the artifact in Arthur's hand, and he was trying to determine, from across the hall, where the artifact ended and the method began. That was the right question, and Arthur noted that Mercer had found it faster than most of the board.

---

**Scene three  the ranking.**

Mercer's team won by score. Arthur's team won by method. The board listed Mercer first because the grading favored completion time. The appendix listed Arthur first because the grading favored reproducibility.

The two lists were read aloud in the same announcement, one after the other, and the hall heard them both. That was the moment Arthur understood what the board had done. They had not chosen between the two readings. They had published both, and left the students to sort it out.

Mercer met him afterward in the corridor.

"You embarrassed me," Mercer said.

"I solved the Seep."

"No. You showed the crowd that your solution required no rank. That means my rank means nothing."

"Your rank means something. It just does not mean you are right."

Mercer stared at him. "I am going to beat you in a real mission. Not a demonstration. A mission where the Seep is not in a crate."

"I know," Arthur said.

"Don't say 'I know.' Say you'll take it seriously."

"I'll take it seriously."

"Good." Mercer turned to go, then stopped. "For what it's worth, the second demonstration was better than the first. The first could have been luck."

"It wasn't."

"I know. That's why I asked for the second."

He walked away, and Arthur filed the exchange under RIVAL, first entry, because it was the first conversation in which Mercer had admitted that the reading might hold.

Clarke was waiting at the end of the corridor with the roster in his hand.

"He was measuring you," Clarke said.

"I know."

"He found the right question faster than the board did. That is not a small thing."

"It is a dangerous thing," Arthur said. "A rival who finds the right question will eventually find the answer. I have to decide what happens when he does."

"You could tell him the answer."

"Then he would have it without earning it, and he would not believe it." Arthur took the roster. "Belief is his power. He has to reach the answer himself, or it will not hold for him."

---

**Scene four  the number.**

That night, Arthur did the arithmetic. Mercer's team had solved their phenomenon in four minutes and scored higher. Arthur's team had solved theirs in forty and scored lower, but reproduced the result. The board's grading formula weighted time and reproducibility equally, which meant the two teams were, by the formula, a draw  and the board had listed Mercer first anyway, because a ranking had to have a first.

The formula was the problem. It assumed that speed and reproducibility measured the same thing. A D-rank could be fast. An F-rank had to be reproducible, because no one would believe a fast answer from him. The formula punished him for the prejudice it was built on.

He wrote that down and put the page in the parallel file, because it was the kind of observation that would be useful in three months. Then he went to bed.

But he did not sleep immediately. He lay in the dark and thought about Mercer's smile, and about the way the room had leaned forward, and about the fact that the board had published both readings instead of choosing one. The board had not been lazy. The board had been honest. They had shown the Academy two ways of measuring a person and let the Academy decide which one to trust.

That was the real contest, and it was not between him and Mercer. It was between the two lists, and the lists would be read by every student in the Academy for the rest of the term. Mercer would spend the term defending his list. Arthur would spend the term making his list reproducible. Neither of them would win. The formula would decide, and the formula was written by people who had never met either of them.

In the morning, the board posted the review. It said what it said. Arthur read it, filed it, and requested the next exercise roster. Mercer's name was on it. So was the ferry district.
