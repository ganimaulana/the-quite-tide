# DIVISION CONFLICT: RESOURCES

**Scene one â€” the ledger.**

The quarterly ledger posted on the first Monday of November, on the board outside the dining hall where every student could read it and most students did. Arthur read it from the bottom because that was where the Irregular cohort had always been. They were not at the bottom anymore.

The stamped-student cluster had the highest inclusion-request rate in the Academy. Other divisions were asking for them on mixed missions. Combat was asking for them, which meant the requests were not favors. A favor could be declined. A request was a form.

He read the number twice. Inclusion-request rate measured how often a division asked for a student by name. The Irregulars had been requested eleven times in the quarter. The next closest cohort had been requested six.

"Material reallocation," Clarke said. He stood beside Arthur with a folder under his arm. "The Logistics section is losing ward-tuned instruments to the Medical annex. The Combat section is losing simulator time to the Tactical wing."

"Why?" Arthur asked.

"Because we exist. The Academy budget is fixed. If one division looks better, another division looks worse."

Arthur looked at the numbers. The Irregulars had not moved up. The other divisions had moved down. That was a different thing than victory. Victory was a number going up. This was a number being taken from someone, and the someone would notice.

"Who draws up the ledger?" he asked.

"The doctrine office. But they draw it from the division reports, and the division reports are written by the divisions." Clarke tapped the page. "The Combat report this quarter was short. Ours was long. The ledger reads length as activity."

"So we made ourselves visible."

"We made ourselves legible. That is the same thing, except you can choose it."

**Scene two â€” the procedural retaliation.**

The filing audit of Irregular missions appeared two days later. It was signed by a Combat section lead and it asked questions about every exercise Arthur had been part of: CH019, CH021, CH022. The questions were neutral. The answers could only be neutral if the Academy was willing to say the Irregulars had done nothing wrong.

Hayes dropped the audit on Arthur's table.

"This is not about the missions," Hayes said.

"I know."

"It is about the ledger. They want the numbers to balance."

"They want us off the board."

Hayes looked at him with the same watchfulness he had shown since the waterfront review. "No. They want you to look ordinary enough that the ledger does not matter."

Arthur read the audit. It was well-written. It asked for records he had already filed, reports he had already written, signatures he could not have forged. Every question had an answer. The audit would find nothing because there was nothing to find.

"That is the point," Hayes said, watching him read. "It is not meant to find anything. It is meant to cost you time. Every hour you spend answering is an hour you do not spend on the next exercise. The Combat section is not attacking your record. It is attacking your calendar."

"That is efficient."

"It is the most efficient way to fight someone whose only weapon is filing. You cannot take away the filing. You can take away the time."

Arthur counted the questions. There were sixty-four. At twenty minutes each, the audit would consume the better part of two weeks.

"Who wrote the questions?" he asked.

"A Combat section lead in the doctrine office. She is careful. Every question is answerable from records you already filed, so you cannot refuse to answer, and every question requires you to look something up, so you cannot answer from memory." Hayes sat down across from him. "That is the design. It is not a trap. It is a tax."

"Then I will pay it."

"You will pay it from your own hours. And you will answer sixty-four questions in two weeks, which means you will have no hours left for the exercise." Hayes stood. "Unless you answer all sixty-four on the first day."

Arthur looked at the audit again. Hayes was right. The questions were answerable from the annex. He knew where every one of them was filed, because he had filed most of them himself. The audit had been designed by someone who assumed the target would work from memory, in the order the questions were asked.

It had not been designed by someone who kept a four-column index.

"First day," Arthur said.

Hayes stopped at the door. "Good," he said, and left.

**Scene three â€” the appendix.**

His method entered the manual as an appendix. Not a main chapter. Not a recommended procedure. An appendix, where things that worked once were kept in case they worked again.

Arthur read it in the doctrine office, standing, because no one had offered him a chair. The words were his, mostly. Someone in the office had flattened them: *adaptive-behavior identification, steady-anchor containment, records-first documentation.* His name appeared once, in a footnote, as the source.

"Appendix is access," Clarke said when Arthur told him. "Appendix is where things go to be cited."

"Appendix is where things go to be ignored."

"The difference is important. A main chapter tells students what to do. An appendix tells them what was done. One is doctrine. The other is evidence." Clarke closed the manual on the page. "You have been turned into evidence. That is not nothing."

Arthur thought about the ledger, the audit, and the footnote. The Academy had put his method in a place where it could be read, and it had removed him from the chapter where it would be followed. He had been recognized and contained in the same act, by the same hand, in the same week.

He closed the manual. The audit would come back clean. The ledger would post again next quarter. The appendix would sit on a shelf until someone needed it.

That was not a victory. That was a place to stand.

Clarke was waiting for him in the corridor with two cups of tea. They drank standing up, because there were no chairs in the corridor.

"You are not angry," Clarke said.

"No."

"You should be. They took your time and gave you a footnote."

"I got the footnote," Arthur said. "The time was already mine. They think they are taking it because they assume I answer questions in order."

Clarke looked at him over the cup. "You are going to index the audit."

"I indexed it this morning."

"Before you read the questions?"

"Before they sent them." Arthur finished the tea. "I have answered sixty-four audit questions before. The last audit had sixty-one. This one has three more, and they are all in the same family. The index holds."

Clarke set down his cup. "That is why you keep the four-column ledger."

"That is why I keep everything." Arthur put his cup on the rack. "The Academy believes records exist to prove what happened. I believe records exist to save time. We are both right, and only one of us is fast."

**Scene four â€” the next quarter.**

That night, he wrote two entries in his notebook. The first was the audit deadline: eleven days. The second was the ledger formula, copied from the doctrine office's own handbook, which Clarke had found in the east wing and which explained, in plain language, exactly how a division report became a division number.

He read the formula three times. It was simpler than he had expected. Inclusion-request rate was not a measure of skill. It was a measure of how often the other divisions had written his cohort's name. The number could be moved by writing. He could move it himself, if he wrote the right reports in the right order.

The formula's second line was the interesting one. Requests by name counted double. Requests by cohort counted once. Most divisions wrote cohort requests because cohort requests were less work. If the Irregulars began writing their own reports carefully enough, other divisions would start naming individual students, because a named student was easier to justify to the board as a cost.

He did not write anything that night. He only wrote down that the number was movable, and that knowing it was movable was the first time in three months he had held something the Academy did not know he held.

Then he closed the notebook, checked the lock on the drawer, and went to bed. In the morning, the first audit question would be waiting, and he would answer it plainly, because answers were free and time was the thing the audit had come to take.

He was asleep before the clock in the corridor struck ten. In the doctrine office across the campus, a Combat section lead he had never met was reading the appendix with his name in the footnote, and deciding whether the footnote had been a mistake. Neither of them knew about the other. Both of them were filing.

**Scene five — the question behind the exercise.**

Arthur found the exercise notice again before leaving the dining hall. One line had been underlined: **all members of the mixed team must submit an individual assessment.**

"You see it too," Clarke said.

Arthur looked up. "The exercise is not only about the team."

"It says individual assessment."

"The less obvious part is why they need it after the audit."

Owen folded his notebook shut. "Maybe they want to know whether the result belongs to the group or to you."

Arthur considered that. The Academy had spent the term testing whether the Irregulars were useful together. The audit had tested whether the artifact was real. The next exercise could test whether the methods survived when the people were separated.

"If they split us," Arthur said, "the question changes."

Clarke nodded. "From can the team solve it to can the Academy explain why the team works."

Arthur disliked that question more than the first one.

He looked across the table. Pram was not there. That bothered him more than it should have. Pram had become useful precisely because he challenged assumptions Arthur would otherwise leave untouched. If the Academy separated them, Arthur would lose a source of resistance as well as a teammate.

Owen noticed the silence. "You are thinking about Pram."

"Yes."

"You could ask him."

Arthur almost did. Then he stopped. The exercise notice had been posted publicly. Pram would see it. If Arthur went looking for him before the test, he would be reacting to the Academy's design before knowing the design.

"No," he said. "Let the exercise tell us what it is first."

Clarke smiled faintly. "That is the most Arthur answer you have given all week."

Arthur folded the notice and put it in his folder.

Tomorrow, the Academy would ask them to perform separately.

He wanted to know what it expected them to fail at.
